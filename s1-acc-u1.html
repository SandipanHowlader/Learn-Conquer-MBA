0. Introduction to Accounting

0.1 Meaning and Definition of Accounting

Accounting is the systematic process of identifying, recording, classifying, summarizing, analyzing and interpreting the financial transactions and events of a business, and communicating the results to interested parties. For a manager, accounting is not merely a record-keeping exercise — it is the "language of business," providing the financial information on which planning, decision-making and control depend.

American Institute of Certified Public Accountants (AICPA): Accounting is the art of recording, classifying and summarizing, in a significant manner and in terms of money, transactions and events which are, in part at least, of a financial character, and interpreting the results thereof.

American Accounting Association (AAA): Accounting is the process of identifying, measuring and communicating economic information to permit informed judgements and decisions by users of the information.

0.2 Objectives of Accounting

  • To maintain a systematic and permanent record of all business transactions.
  • To ascertain the profit or loss of the business for a given accounting period.
  • To ascertain the financial position (assets, liabilities and capital) of the business at a given point in time.
  • To provide financial information to management, owners, and other stakeholders for decision-making.
  • To ensure control over assets and prevent/detect errors and fraud.
  • To fulfil statutory/legal and tax-reporting requirements.

0.3 Users of Accounting Information

Internal Users External Users
Owners/Management, Department Heads, Employees Investors and Shareholders, Creditors and Lenders, Government and Tax Authorities, Customers, Regulators, Researchers and the Public

1. Fundamentals of Accounting

Before studying accounting techniques, a manager must be familiar with a few basic terms and the fundamental accounting equation that underlies every recorded transaction.

1.1 Basic Accounting Terms

  • Assets — economic resources owned by the business that are expected to provide future benefit (e.g., cash, buildings, machinery, debtors).
  • Liabilities — obligations or amounts owed by the business to outsiders (e.g., creditors, loans, bank overdraft).
  • Capital (Owner's Equity) — the owner's claim on the business, i.e., the amount invested by the owner plus accumulated profits, less drawings and losses.
  • Revenue — the income earned by a business from its normal operating activities, chiefly the sale of goods or services.
  • Expense — the cost incurred by a business in earning revenue during an accounting period.
  • Drawings — cash or goods withdrawn by the owner from the business for personal use.
  • Debtors — persons or entities who owe money to the business (typically for goods sold on credit).
  • Creditors — persons or entities to whom the business owes money (typically for goods bought on credit).

1.2 The Fundamental Accounting Equation

Every recorded transaction affects this equation in a way that keeps both sides equal — this is the foundation of the double-entry system of bookkeeping.

Fig. 1 — What a business owns (Assets) is always financed either by what it owes to outsiders (Liabilities) or by what the owner has invested (Capital).

2. Branches of Accounting

As the needs of different users of financial information diversified, accounting evolved into several specialized branches, each with its own concept, importance and scope.

Fig. 2 — The three branches build on one another: financial accounting provides the base data, cost accounting refines it for cost control, and management accounting uses both to support decision-making.

2.1 Financial Accounting

Concept: Financial accounting is concerned with the recording of business transactions, and the periodic preparation of financial statements — the Profit & Loss Account (Income Statement) and the Balance Sheet — that show the profitability and financial position of the business.
Importance: Provides a historical record of transactions; forms the basis for statutory/legal reporting and taxation; enables external stakeholders (investors, banks, government) to assess the enterprise.
Scope: Covers the entire enterprise as a whole, is largely historical (records what has already happened), and is governed by Generally Accepted Accounting Principles and Accounting Standards.

2.2 Cost Accounting

Concept: Cost accounting is concerned with recording, classifying, and analyzing the costs of materials, labour and overheads to ascertain the cost of a product, process, job or service.
Importance: Helps in cost control and cost reduction; assists in fixing selling prices; provides data for inventory valuation.
Scope: Focuses on individual products, departments or cost centres, and can be both historical and predictive (standard costing, budgeting).

2.3 Management Accounting

Concept: Management accounting uses both financial and cost accounting data, reshaping and analyzing it to assist management in planning, decision-making and control. It is entirely internal and future-oriented.
Importance: Supports budgeting, forecasting, variance analysis and strategic decision-making; presents information in whatever form is most useful to a specific manager, without being bound by external reporting rules.
Scope: Not governed by any single set of external rules; the format and content are tailored entirely to management's internal needs.

3. Business Entities

Accounting treats every business as an entity separate from its owner(s) — the Business Entity Concept discussed further in Section 5. The legal form the business takes affects how its accounts are prepared and reported.

Form of Entity Ownership Liability Legal Status
Sole ProprietorshipOne individualUnlimitedNo separate legal identity from the owner
PartnershipTwo or more partnersUnlimited (joint and several)No separate legal identity in most jurisdictions (except LLPs)
Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)Two or more partnersLimited to their contributionSeparate legal entity
Company / CorporationShareholdersLimited to the value of shares heldSeparate legal entity, distinct from its owners

Regardless of legal form, accounting always records the transactions of the business separately from the personal transactions of its owner(s) — a distinction that becomes especially important, and legally reinforced, in the case of companies.

4. Accounting Transactions

4.1 Meaning of a Transaction

An accounting transaction is any event that has a financial impact on the business and can be measured reliably in monetary terms, and which is therefore recorded in the books of account. Only events with a financial character are recorded — e.g., hiring a well-liked employee is a significant business event but is not, by itself, an accounting transaction, whereas paying that employee's salary is.

4.2 Types of Transactions

  • Cash Transactions — where payment/receipt happens immediately in cash or cash-equivalents.
  • Credit Transactions — where the payment/receipt is deferred to a later date.
  • Internal Transactions — events that do not involve an external party (e.g., depreciation, transfer between departments).
  • External Transactions — events involving another business or individual outside the entity (e.g., a sale to a customer, purchase from a supplier).

4.3 Source Documents

Every transaction is first evidenced by a source document — such as an invoice, receipt, cheque, or voucher — which forms the primary basis on which the transaction is recorded in the books of account.

5. Accounting Concepts and Conventions

Accounting concepts and conventions are the basic assumptions and customs that underlie the preparation of financial statements, ensuring that accounting information is prepared on a consistent, comparable and reliable basis across different businesses and time periods.

Fig. 3 — Nine fundamental accounting concepts, each addressing a different assumption needed to prepare consistent and meaningful financial statements.

5.1 Fundamental Accounting Concepts

  • Business Entity Concept — the business is treated as an entity distinct and separate from its owner(s); only the business's own transactions are recorded in its books.
  • Money Measurement Concept — only those transactions and events that can be expressed in monetary terms are recorded in accounting.
  • Going Concern Concept — the business is assumed to continue its operations for the foreseeable future, unless there is clear evidence to the contrary.
  • Cost Concept (Historical Cost) — assets are recorded in the books at their original purchase cost, not at current market value.
  • Dual Aspect Concept — every transaction has two aspects (a debit and a corresponding credit), forming the basis of the double-entry system and the accounting equation.
  • Accounting Period Concept — the indefinite life of a business is divided into shorter, consistent periods (usually one year) for the purpose of measuring profit and preparing financial statements.
  • Matching Concept — expenses incurred in an accounting period should be matched against the revenue earned in the same period, to correctly determine profit.
  • Realisation Concept — revenue is recognized only when it is actually earned (e.g., when goods are delivered or services rendered), not merely when cash is received.
  • Accrual Concept — transactions are recorded as they occur (when revenue is earned or expense is incurred), regardless of when the actual cash is received or paid.

5.2 Accounting Conventions

  • Convention of Consistency — the same accounting methods and policies should be followed from one period to the next, so that financial statements remain comparable over time.
  • Convention of Conservatism (Prudence) — anticipate no profit, but provide for all possible losses; when in doubt, record the more cautious/lower figure (e.g., valuing stock at the lower of cost or market value).
  • Convention of Full Disclosure — financial statements should disclose all material information necessary to make them not misleading to users.
  • Convention of Materiality — only information that is significant enough to influence the decisions of users needs to be disclosed separately; insignificant items can be aggregated or ignored.

6. Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)

6.1 Meaning

Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) refer to the common set of accounting concepts, conventions, standards, and procedures that are widely accepted and followed for preparing and presenting financial statements, so that they are comparable, reliable and understandable across different organizations.

6.2 Features of GAAP

  • Developed over time through accounting practice, professional pronouncements, and regulatory requirements.
  • Not a single, rigid rulebook but a broad framework built from the fundamental accounting concepts and conventions covered in Section 5.
  • Ensures uniformity and comparability of financial statements across companies and time periods.
  • Continuously evolves and is refined through formal Accounting Standards (see Section 7).

Relationship to concepts and conventions: GAAP is essentially the umbrella term for the entire body of accounting concepts and conventions (Section 5) taken together, formalized further through Accounting Standards (Section 7) issued by professional and regulatory bodies.

Fig. 4 — Broad, general concepts and conventions are progressively codified into GAAP, then into formal, detailed Accounting Standards such as Ind AS or IFRS.

7. Concept of Accounting Standards

7.1 Meaning and Need

Accounting Standards are formal, written policy documents issued by a recognized expert accounting body (such as a national Accounting Standards Board or an international body) that specify how particular types of transactions and events should be recognized, measured, presented and disclosed in financial statements.

Accounting Standards exist because GAAP alone, being a broad and somewhat flexible set of concepts and conventions, still leaves room for significant variation in how different businesses might treat the same type of transaction. Standards narrow this room for variation, making financial statements genuinely comparable.

7.2 Objectives of Accounting Standards

  • To harmonize the diverse accounting policies and practices followed by different enterprises.
  • To improve the reliability and comparability of financial statements across companies and industries.
  • To reduce or eliminate confusing accounting alternatives, so that "creative" or misleading accounting is minimized.
  • To assist auditors in forming an opinion on whether financial statements give a true and fair view.
  • To build confidence among investors, creditors and other users of financial statements.

7.3 Standard-Setting Bodies

  • International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) — issued by the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB), adopted or converged with in most countries around the world.
  • Indian Accounting Standards (Ind AS) — issued in India under the supervision of the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI) through its Accounting Standards Board (ASB), and notified by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs; Ind AS are converged with IFRS.
  • Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (US GAAP) — the detailed, codified standards followed in the United States, issued by the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB).

In India, companies apply either the older set of "Accounting Standards (AS)" or the newer, IFRS-converged "Indian Accounting Standards (Ind AS)," depending on their size and listing status, as prescribed by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs.

Summary

This unit introduced accounting as the systematic process of recording, classifying, summarizing and communicating an organization's financial information, and set out its objectives and the internal and external users who rely on it. It covered the basic terms of accounting — assets, liabilities, capital, revenue, expenses, debtors and creditors — and the fundamental accounting equation (Assets = Liabilities + Capital) that underlies the double-entry system. The unit explained the three main branches of accounting — financial, cost and management accounting — comparing their concept, importance and scope, and outlined the major forms of business entities (sole proprietorship, partnership, LLP and company) and how their legal status shapes accounting treatment. It defined an accounting transaction and its types, before covering the fundamental accounting concepts (business entity, money measurement, going concern, cost, dual aspect, accounting period, matching, realisation and accrual) and the key accounting conventions (consistency, conservatism, full disclosure and materiality) that together form the foundation of Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP). Finally, the unit explained the concept, need and objectives of formal Accounting Standards, and the role of bodies such as the ICAI's Accounting Standards Board, IFRS and Ind AS in harmonizing accounting practice and making financial statements genuinely comparable.
