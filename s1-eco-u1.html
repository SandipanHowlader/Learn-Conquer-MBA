0. Introduction to Managerial Economics

0.1 Meaning and Scope

Managerial economics is the application of economic theory and quantitative methods to the analysis of business decision-making problems. It bridges traditional economic theory and actual managerial practice, helping managers make optimal decisions about pricing, output, resource allocation and investment under conditions of scarcity and uncertainty.

Spencer and Siegelman: Managerial economics is the integration of economic theory with business practice for the purpose of facilitating decision-making and forward planning by management.

0.2 Basic Economic Concepts

  • Scarcity — resources (time, money, materials) are limited relative to unlimited human wants, making choice unavoidable (explored further in Section 1).
  • Opportunity Cost — the value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made (introduced formally in Unit 5 of Accounting for Managers as a decision-making cost).
  • Marginal Analysis — examining the effect of a small (one-unit) change in one variable on another; the basis of most optimization decisions in economics.
  • Equi-Marginal Principle — resources should be allocated across alternative uses so that the marginal benefit per unit of resource is equal in every use (elaborated in Section 6.3).
  • Time Perspective — economic decisions must distinguish between the short run (some factors fixed) and the long run (all factors variable).
  • Discounting Principle — a rupee receivable in the future is worth less than a rupee in hand today, since money has a time value.

1. Choice as an Economic Problem

1.1 Scarcity and the Need to Choose

The fundamental economic problem facing every individual, firm and economy is that resources are scarce (limited) while wants are virtually unlimited. Because not everything that is wanted can be produced or consumed, every economic agent is forced to choose which wants will be satisfied and which will remain unmet — choice, and the opportunity cost that comes with it, is therefore the very essence of economics.

1.2 The Central Problems of an Economy

  • What to produce — which goods and services, and in what quantities, should be produced with the available scarce resources?
  • How to produce — which technique or combination of resources (labour-intensive or capital-intensive) should be used?
  • For whom to produce — how should the resulting output be distributed among the members of society?

1.3 The Production Possibility Curve (PPC)

The Production Possibility Curve (or Frontier) illustrates the concept of choice and opportunity cost graphically. It shows the maximum possible combinations of two goods that an economy (or firm) can produce with its given resources and technology, assuming full and efficient utilization of those resources.

Fig. 1 — Producing more of Good X (moving along the curve from left to right) requires giving up some quantity of Good Y — the curve itself is a picture of opportunity cost.

2. Concept of Decision-Making

2.1 Meaning of Managerial Decision-Making

Managerial decision-making is the process of selecting the best available course of action from among competing alternatives, in order to achieve a specific managerial objective, subject to the constraints imposed by scarce resources. This process, its rationality, and its key steps were examined in detail in Unit 3 of Management Principles and Application; here the focus is specifically on how the economic conditions surrounding a decision shape the approach a manager must take.

2.2 Decision-Making Under Different Conditions

3. Theory of Demand

3.1 Meaning of Demand

In economics, demand refers to the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to purchase at a given price, during a given period of time. Both willingness and ability to pay are essential — a want that is not backed by purchasing power is not "demand" in the economic sense.

3.2 The Law of Demand

The law of demand states that, other things remaining constant (the "ceteris paribus" condition), the quantity demanded of a good is inversely related to its price — as price rises, quantity demanded falls, and as price falls, quantity demanded rises.

3.4 Exceptions to the Law of Demand

  • Giffen Goods — certain inferior goods (essential to the very poor) where a fall in price can actually reduce quantity demanded, because the resulting rise in real income leads consumers to substitute toward a more preferred good.
  • Veblen (Conspicuous Consumption) Goods — luxury goods bought partly for their high price as a status symbol, where demand can rise as price rises.
  • Speculative Demand — if consumers expect prices to rise further, they may buy more even at a current higher price (e.g., stocks, real estate, or essential goods during expected shortages).

4. Demand Function

4.1 Meaning and General Form

A demand function expresses the quantity demanded of a good as a mathematical function of all the variables that influence it, not price alone.

Qdx = f(Px, Py, Y, T, E, N, A, ...)

where: Px = price of the good itself; Py = price of related goods (substitutes/complements); Y = consumer income; T = tastes and preferences; E = expected future price; N = number of buyers; A = advertising/promotional expenditure.

Worked Example: If the demand function for Good X is Qdx = 500 − 5Px, then at a price of ₹50, quantity demanded = 500 − (5 × 50) = 500 − 250 = 250 units — the same figure used to build the demand schedule and curve in Section 3.3.

5. Elasticity of Demand

5.1 Meaning

Elasticity of demand measures the degree of responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in one of its determinants — price, income, or the price of a related good — expressed as a ratio of percentage changes, which makes it a "unit-free" measure comparable across very different goods.

5.2 Types of Elasticity of Demand

  • Price Elasticity of Demand (Ed) — responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the good's own price.
  • Income Elasticity of Demand (Ey) — responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in consumer income; positive for normal goods, negative for inferior goods.
  • Cross Elasticity of Demand (Exy) — responsiveness of the quantity demanded of one good to a change in the price of another; positive for substitutes, negative for complements.

5.3 Degrees of Price Elasticity

Fig. 3 — The flatter the demand curve, the more elastic (responsive) demand is to a change in price.

5.4 Measurement of Price Elasticity

(a) Percentage (Proportionate) Method:

Ed = (% Change in Quantity Demanded) ÷ (% Change in Price)

Worked Example: When the price of Good X falls from ₹50 to ₹40, quantity demanded rises from 250 to 300 units.

% Change in Quantity Demanded = (300 − 250) ÷ 250 × 100+20%
% Change in Price = (40 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100−20%
Ed = 20% ÷ (−20%)−1 (i.e., |Ed| = 1, Unit Elastic)

(b) Total Outlay (Total Expenditure) Method: Observe how total consumer expenditure (Price × Quantity) changes as price changes.

Price (₹)QuantityTotal Outlay (₹)
201002,000
151502,250

Interpretation: As price fell from ₹20 to ₹15, total outlay rose from ₹2,000 to ₹2,250 — whenever a price fall increases total outlay (or a price rise decreases it), demand is elastic (Ed > 1) over that range. If outlay had fallen along with price, demand would be inelastic; if outlay had stayed unchanged, demand would be unit elastic.

5.5 Factors Affecting Price Elasticity of Demand

  • Availability of substitutes — the more close substitutes a good has, the more elastic its demand.
  • Nature of the good — necessities tend to have inelastic demand; luxuries tend to have elastic demand.
  • Proportion of income spent — goods that take up a larger share of the consumer's budget tend to have more elastic demand.
  • Time period — demand tends to be more elastic in the long run, as consumers have more time to adjust their behaviour.
  • Number of uses of the good — a good with many alternative uses tends to have more elastic demand.

6. Cardinal Utility Theory

6.1 Meaning of Utility and Assumptions

Utility is the satisfaction a consumer derives from consuming a good or service. The cardinal utility approach (associated with Alfred Marshall) assumes that utility can be measured numerically, in units called "utils," and that the marginal utility of money itself remains constant, allowing a consumer's satisfaction to be added, subtracted and compared across goods.

6.2 Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility

The law of diminishing marginal utility states that as a consumer consumes successive additional units of a good (holding consumption of all other goods constant), the additional (marginal) utility derived from each successive unit tends to decline.

6.3 Law of Equi-Marginal Utility (Consumer Equilibrium)

A rational consumer, spending a fixed income across two or more goods, maximizes total satisfaction by allocating that income so that the marginal utility per rupee spent is equal across all goods purchased.

Consumer Equilibrium Condition: MUx ÷ Px = MUy ÷ Py = Marginal Utility of Money

7. Indifference Curve Theory (Ordinal Utility Approach)

7.1 Meaning and Assumptions

The indifference curve approach, developed by J.R. Hicks and R.G.D. Allen, avoids the assumption that utility can be measured numerically. Instead, it assumes only that a consumer can rank (order) different combinations of two goods according to preference — an "ordinal" rather than "cardinal" measure of satisfaction.

7.2 Properties of Indifference Curves

  • An indifference curve shows all combinations of two goods that give the consumer an equal level of total satisfaction, so the consumer is indifferent between any two points on the same curve.
  • Indifference curves slope downward from left to right, reflecting the fact that more of one good must be compensated by less of the other to keep satisfaction constant.
  • Indifference curves are convex to the origin, reflecting the diminishing marginal rate of substitution between the two goods.
  • Higher indifference curves represent higher levels of satisfaction, and indifference curves belonging to the same consumer can never intersect.

7.3 The Budget Line

The budget line (or price line) shows all the combinations of two goods that a consumer can just afford to buy, given their income and the prices of the two goods. Its slope reflects the ratio of the two goods' prices (Px ÷ Py).

7.4 Consumer Equilibrium (Tangency Condition)

Fig. 5 — Consumer equilibrium occurs where the budget line is exactly tangent to the highest indifference curve the consumer can reach (IC₂ at point E) — any higher curve (IC₃) is unaffordable, and any lower one (IC₁) leaves satisfaction unnecessarily low.

At the equilibrium point, the slope of the indifference curve (the Marginal Rate of Substitution, MRSxy) exactly equals the slope of the budget line (the price ratio Px ÷ Py) — the consumer cannot reach a higher indifference curve without violating the budget constraint.

8. Consumer Surplus

8.1 Meaning

Consumer surplus, a concept developed by Alfred Marshall, is the difference between the maximum price a consumer is willing and able to pay for a given quantity of a good (as shown by the demand curve) and the price they actually have to pay (the market price). It is a monetary measure of the net benefit consumers gain from being able to buy a good at the market price.

8.2 Worked Example

Using the demand function from Section 4 (Qd = 500 − 5P, so the inverse demand curve is P = 100 − 0.2Q) and a market price of ₹50 (at which Qd = 250 units, as calculated in Section 4.1):

Consumer Surplus = ½ × Base × Height = ½ × Quantity × (Choke Price − Market Price)
Consumer Surplus = ½ × 250 × (100 − 50) = ½ × 250 × 50 = ₹6,250

This ₹6,250 represents the total extra value consumers collectively receive — because most units were valued by consumers at a price above ₹50 (as shown by the demand curve in Fig. 2), yet everyone pays only the uniform market price of ₹50.

9. Demand and Price: Managerial Applications

The theory of demand covered in Sections 3–8 is not merely academic — it directly informs several practical managerial challenges:

  • Pricing decisions — a manager facing inelastic demand (Section 5.3) can raise price to increase total revenue; a manager facing elastic demand should generally avoid price increases, or consider price cuts to boost revenue.
  • Price discrimination — knowledge of different elasticities across customer segments (e.g., business vs. leisure air travellers) allows firms to charge different prices to different groups, capturing more of the consumer surplus described in Section 8.
  • Product bundling and discounting — understanding the shape of the demand curve and consumer surplus helps managers judge how much a promotional discount will actually increase sales volume.
  • Tax incidence and subsidy design — the relative price elasticity of demand and supply determines how much of a tax burden (or subsidy benefit) is passed on to consumers versus absorbed by producers.
  • New product forecasting — estimating a demand function (Section 4) for a new or existing product allows a manager to simulate the revenue impact of different pricing strategies before committing to one.

10. Derivation of Market Demand

10.1 Meaning: Horizontal Summation

Market demand is the total quantity of a good that all buyers in the market are willing and able to purchase at each possible price. It is derived by adding together, at each price level, the individual quantities demanded by every buyer in the market — a process called horizontal summation, since quantities (the horizontal axis) are added at each given price.

Fig. 6 — At every given price, the market demand curve's quantity is simply the sum of every individual buyer's quantity demanded at that same price.

11. Determinants of Demand

  • Price of the good itself — the basis of the law of demand (Section 3.2).
  • Income of consumers — demand for normal goods rises with income; demand for inferior goods falls with income.
  • Price of related goods — a rise in the price of a substitute increases demand for the good in question; a rise in the price of a complement decreases it.
  • Tastes and preferences — shaped by fashion, advertising, and changing consumer habits.
  • Consumer expectations — expectations of future price rises or shortages can increase current demand.
  • Population and number of buyers — a larger market generally means higher total demand.
  • Advertising and promotional expenditure — can shift the entire demand curve outward by influencing tastes and awareness.
  • Government policy — taxes, subsidies, and regulations can raise or lower the effective price faced by consumers.

12. Overview of Demand Forecasting

12.1 Meaning and Need

Demand forecasting is the process of estimating the likely future demand for a product or service, providing the foundation for a firm's production planning, budgeting, inventory management (Unit 5 of Accounting for Managers), staffing and financial planning.

13. Numerical Problems Related to Management

The following additional problems draw together the concepts covered in this unit.

Problem 1 (Demand Function): If Qd = 800 − 4P, find the quantity demanded when price = ₹100.
Solution: Qd = 800 − (4 × 100) = 800 − 400 = 400 units.

Problem 2 (Price Elasticity): When the price of a good rises from ₹25 to ₹30, quantity demanded falls from 400 to 340 units. Calculate the price elasticity of demand and state whether demand is elastic or inelastic.
Solution: % Change in Quantity = (340 − 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = −15%. % Change in Price = (30 − 25) ÷ 25 × 100 = +20%. Ed = −15% ÷ 20% = −0.75 (|Ed| = 0.75 < 1, so demand is relatively inelastic).

Problem 3 (Consumer Surplus): If the inverse demand curve is P = 60 − 0.5Q and the market price is ₹20, find the consumer surplus.
Solution: At P = 20: 20 = 60 − 0.5Q → Q = 80 units. Consumer Surplus = ½ × 80 × (60 − 20) = ½ × 80 × 40 = ₹1,600.

Summary

This unit introduced managerial economics as the application of economic theory to business decision-making, starting from the basic economic concepts of scarcity, opportunity cost, and marginal and equi-marginal analysis. It explained choice as the fundamental economic problem, illustrated through the central "what, how, for whom" questions and the Production Possibility Curve, and linked managerial decision-making to the conditions of certainty, risk and uncertainty covered more fully in Management Principles and Application. The unit then built a complete picture of demand theory: the law of demand and its exceptions (Giffen and Veblen goods), the demand function and its determinants, and elasticity of demand (price, income and cross elasticity, its five degrees, and the percentage and total outlay methods of measurement). It covered both major theories of consumer behaviour — cardinal utility theory (diminishing marginal utility and the equi-marginal principle) and the ordinal indifference curve approach (properties of indifference curves, the budget line, and the tangency condition for consumer equilibrium) — and explained consumer surplus as the monetary value of the gap between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay. Finally, it connected this theory to managerial practice through applications in pricing and price discrimination, explained how individual demand curves are horizontally summed into market demand, listed the key determinants of demand, and gave an overview of survey-based and statistical methods of demand forecasting — closing with fully worked numerical problems drawing the unit's concepts together.
