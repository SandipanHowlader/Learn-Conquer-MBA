1. Planning and Types of Plans

1.1 Concept, Characteristics, and Limitations of Planning

Planning is the most fundamental and primary function of management. It is a highly intellectual process that bridges the gap from where an organization currently is to where it wants to be in the future.

Planning involves deciding in advance what to do, how to do it, when to do it, and who is to do it. Because the future is highly uncertain, planning requires foresight, forecasting, and rational decision-making based on "planning premises" (assumptions about the future environment).

Key Characteristics of Planning:

  • Primacy: It precedes all other managerial functions (Organizing, Staffing, Directing, Controlling). You cannot organize resources without a plan.
  • Pervasiveness: Planning is required at all levels of management, from the CEO (strategic planning) down to the shift supervisor (operational planning).
  • Forward-Looking: It is fundamentally about anticipating the future.
  • Decision-Making: Planning inherently involves making choices between alternative courses of action.

Limitations of Planning:

  • Creates Rigidity: Once a detailed plan is set, managers may blindly follow it even when the external environment changes drastically.
  • Time-Consuming & Costly: Gathering data, forecasting, and holding strategy meetings consume massive amounts of executive time and money.
  • False Sense of Security: A brilliant plan on paper does not guarantee success. Execution is paramount.

1.2 Types of Plans (Standing vs. Single-Use)

Plans are typically classified into a hierarchy. Broadly, they fall into two categories: Standing Plans (used repeatedly for recurring situations, like policies and rules) and Single-Use Plans (developed for a one-time project, like programs and budgets). The 8 primary types of plans are:

Fig. 1 — Plans cascade from broad, philosophical missions down to highly specific, single-use budgets.

  • Missions (or Purposes): The fundamental, unique purpose that sets a business apart and identifies the scope of its operations. It answers the existential question, "What business are we in?"
    Example: Google’s mission is "to organize the world's information and make it universally accessible and useful."
  • Objectives (or Goals): The end points towards which all management activities are directed. They represent the "what" that needs to be achieved.
    Example: "To increase market share in the APAC region by 15% by Q4 2026."
  • Strategies: Broad, long-term action plans designed to achieve objectives in a competitive environment. They dictate how resources will be deployed to gain a competitive advantage. (e.g., A cost-leadership strategy vs. a differentiation strategy).
  • Policies (Standing Plan): General statements or understandings that guide or channel thinking in decision-making. They establish boundaries within which managers can use their discretion.
    Example: "We prefer to promote from within whenever possible" or "We match competitor pricing."
  • Procedures (Standing Plan): Customary, chronological methods of handling future activities. While policies guide thinking, procedures guide action.
    Example: The step-by-step 5-stage process a customer service agent must follow to process a product refund.
  • Rules (Standing Plan): Specific, rigid dictates that require a specific action to be taken or not taken. They allow for zero managerial discretion.
    Example: "Safety goggles must be worn at all times on the factory floor. No exceptions."
  • Programs (Single-Use Plan): A complex network of goals, policies, procedures, rules, task assignments, and resource allocations designed to carry out a major course of action.
    Example: A comprehensive program to build and launch a new EV manufacturing plant in Texas over 3 years.
  • Budgets (Single-Use Plan): A statement of expected results expressed in numerical terms (financial or non-financial). It is a "quantified plan" that acts as both a planning and a controlling device. Note: Modern firms often use Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB) where every expense must be justified for each new period, starting from zero.

1.3 Comparison: Policies vs. Procedures vs. Rules

Feature Policy Procedure Rule
Nature Guide to thinking and decision-making. Guide to action (chronological steps). Rigid instruction for a specific situation.
Flexibility High (allows managerial discretion based on the situation). Low (must follow the specific sequence of steps). None (zero discretion allowed).
MBA Example "We empower employees to ensure customer satisfaction." Step 1: Apologize. Step 2: Issue return label. Step 3: Refund card. "Transactions over $500 require a manager's override PIN."

2. Objectives and Management by Objectives (MBO)

2.1 Hierarchy of Objectives

Objectives form a Means-Ends Chain, cascading down from top to bottom. Achievement of lower-level objectives provides the means to achieve higher-level ends. This hierarchy ensures organizational alignment.

  1. Socio-Economic Purpose: Broadest aim (e.g., societal contribution, sustainability).
  2. Mission: The organization's specific purpose and business scope.
  3. Overall Strategic Objectives: Long-term C-Suite goals (e.g., massive ROI, global market dominance).
  4. Divisional Objectives: Medium-term goals (e.g., "European division to increase sales by 10%").
  5. Departmental Objectives: Short-term goals (e.g., "Marketing to lower customer acquisition cost").
  6. Individual Objectives: Performance targets for individual employees.

2.2 Types of Objectives

Verifiable vs. Non-Verifiable Objectives

Verifiable Objectives: Expressed in a way that allows objective determination of achievement at the end of the period. They leave no room for debate and heavily rely on SMART criteria (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound).
Example: "Reduce production waste to exactly 10% by Dec 31st, 2026."

Non-Verifiable Objectives: Broad, subjective statements of intent, highly difficult to measure or use in performance appraisals.
Example: "Improve employee morale." (An MBA manager must translate this into a verifiable metric, like raising the Employee Net Promoter Score from 40 to 60, to be useful).

Quantitative vs. Qualitative Objectives:

  • Quantitative: Expressed in absolute numbers, percentages, or financial figures (e.g., $10M revenue, 5,000 units produced).
  • Qualitative: Intangible goals related to capability, quality, or reputation (e.g., fostering an innovative corporate culture, improving brand equity).

2.3 Management by Objectives (MBO)

Coined by Peter Drucker in his 1954 book The Practice of Management, MBO represents a massive paradigm shift from traditional top-down, autocratic goal setting to a participative, democratic approach.

The Core Philosophy: Instead of goals being dictated to employees, superiors and subordinates collaborate to jointly define goals and areas of responsibility. This shifts the focus from managing activities to managing results, creating massive psychological buy-in and ownership among employees.

Fig. 2 — MBO is a continuous, collaborative loop rather than a top-down directive.

Advantages of MBO:

  • Better and more precise planning due to verifiable goals.
  • Highly motivating because employees feel ownership of goals they helped create.
  • Provides purely objective, results-based criteria for performance appraisal, removing manager bias.

Limitations of MBO:

  • Short-Termism: Can lead to an over-focus on short-term, quantifiable goals at the expense of long-term qualitative health (e.g., cutting R&D to hit this quarter's profit target).
  • Cultural Resistance: Requires a massive cultural shift towards trust. It fails utterly in highly autocratic corporate cultures.
  • Paperwork Heavy: Can devolve into a bureaucratic exercise of filling out forms rather than genuine strategic alignment.

3. Nature and Purpose of Strategies & Strategic Intent

3.1 Strategies vs. Policies

  • Strategies are dynamic, long-term, and forward-looking action plans concerned with the external competitive environment. Their core purpose is to deploy resources to build a sustainable competitive advantage over rivals.
  • Policies are tactical, internal, standing plans. Their purpose is to ensure consistency in decision-making and act as time-savers (so a manager doesn't have to rethink a problem from scratch every time it occurs).

3.2 Strategic Intent

Popularized by Gary Hamel and C.K. Prahalad, Strategic Intent is a firm’s long-term, borderline unrealistic obsession with winning at all levels of the organization. It purposefully outpaces the firm's current resources and capabilities, forcing the company to heavily innovate to close the gap.

Hamel and Prahalad used two key concepts here: "Stretch" (the massive gap between current resources and the strategic intent) and "Leverage" (innovatively concentrating those limited resources to achieve the impossible). A classic example was Komatsu’s strategic intent to "Encircle Caterpillar" when Caterpillar was massively larger.

Strategic intent forms a hierarchy:

  • Vision: The highly aspirational "guiding star" (e.g., Microsoft’s original vision: "A computer on every desk and in every home").
  • Mission: The current business scope driving toward the vision.
  • Business Definition: Defining the specific customer groups being satisfied, the needs being met, and the technologies/competencies being used.
  • Goals and Objectives: The concrete, time-bound stepping stones to achieve the above.

4. The TOWS Matrix

While most undergraduate students learn SWOT analysis (a simple list of Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats), the TOWS Matrix (developed by Heinz Weihrich) goes a critical step further. It is a strategy generation tool, not just an analytical list. It forces managers to match internal capabilities with external realities to create actionable business plans.

Fig. 3 — TOWS takes the 4 SWOT elements and forces them to intersect, proactively generating actionable strategies.

External Opportunities (O) External Threats (T)
Internal Strengths (S) SO Strategies (Maxi-Maxi)
The most desirable position. Aggressively use massive internal strengths to capitalize on new external opportunities.

MBA Case: Apple using its massive cash reserves and brand loyalty (S) to acquire AI startups in a booming AI tech market (O).		 ST Strategies (Maxi-Mini)
Use internal strengths to avoid, mitigate, or confront external threats.

MBA Case: A dominant legacy bank using its immense regulatory experience and capital (S) to fend off agile new FinTech startup competitors (T).
Internal Weaknesses (W) WO Strategies (Mini-Maxi)
Improve or overcome internal weaknesses by taking advantage of external opportunities.

MBA Case: A traditional automaker with weak software skills (W) forming a joint venture with Google (O) to build autonomous self-driving cars.		 WT Strategies (Mini-Mini)
Defensive tactics aimed at reducing internal weaknesses and avoiding external threats. This is pure survival mode.

MBA Case: A struggling airline with high debt and aging planes (W) liquidating an unprofitable division and freezing hiring during a global economic recession (T).

5. Blue Ocean Strategy

Coined by W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne in 2005, the Blue Ocean Strategy is a massive paradigm shift. It challenges traditional competitive models (like Michael Porter’s Five Forces) by stating that instead of battling competitors in an existing, blood-red, saturated market, companies should innovate to create new, uncontested market space.

5.1 Red Oceans vs. Blue Oceans

Red Ocean Strategy (Traditional) Blue Ocean Strategy (Innovative)
Compete in existing market space. Create uncontested market space.
Beat the competition. Make the competition completely irrelevant.
Exploit existing demand. Create and capture new demand.
Make the value-cost trade-off (Choose between being a premium/high-value brand OR a low-cost brand). Break the value-cost trade-off (Simultaneously achieve high differentiation AND low cost via Value Innovation).

5.2 The Four Actions Framework (ERRC Grid) & Value Innovation

The cornerstone of Blue Ocean Strategy is Value Innovation. To achieve this and map out a new "Strategy Canvas," companies must ask four critical questions about the industry's historical logic using the ERRC Grid:

Fig. 4 — The ERRC grid forces companies to simultaneously break the value-cost trade-off by raising/creating value while eliminating/reducing costs.

  • Eliminate: Which factors that the industry has long competed on (and cost money) should be completely eliminated because they no longer provide real value?
  • Reduce: Which factors should be reduced well below the industry standard?
  • Raise: Which factors should be raised well above the industry standard?
  • Create: Which factors should be created that the industry has never offered before?

5.3 Classic MBA Case Studies

  • Cirque du Soleil: Eliminated costly animal acts, multi-ring stages, and star performers (drastically cutting operational costs). Created artistic original music, professional dance, a cohesive storyline, and upscale theater environments (drastically raising value). Result: They stopped competing with traditional circuses for children. They created a massive new market of high-end, theatre-going adults willing to pay Broadway-level prices.
  • Nintendo Wii: Reduced cutting-edge, expensive graphics processors and complex controllers that Sony (PlayStation) and Microsoft (Xbox) were bleeding money fighting over. Created simple motion-control and family-friendly party games. Result: They stopped competing for hardcore gamers and captured an uncontested non-gamer market (grandparents, young children, fitness enthusiasts).
  • Yellow Tail Wine: Eliminated aging qualities, complex wine terminology, and elite vineyard prestige. Created approachability, ease of selection (only two types initially: a red and a white), and fun, vibrant branding (a kangaroo). Result: They bypassed the snobby, crowded wine market and targeted beer and cocktail drinkers, becoming the fastest-growing imported wine in US history.

Review Questions

Short Answer Type

  1. Distinguish between policies, procedures, and rules with suitable business examples.
  2. What is the fundamental difference between a verifiable and a non-verifiable objective?
  3. Define Strategic Intent. What do Hamel and Prahalad mean by "stretch" and "leverage"?
  4. How does a TOWS Matrix differ from a standard SWOT analysis?
  5. Define "Value Innovation" in the context of Blue Ocean Strategy.

Long Answer / Essay Type

  1. "Planning is the most fundamental of all management functions, yet it has severe limitations." Discuss this statement and classify the various types of standing and single-use plans.
  2. Critically evaluate Peter Drucker's concept of Management by Objectives (MBO). What is the process, and why does it sometimes fail in highly autocratic corporate cultures?
  3. Using the TOWS matrix, explain how a company can match its internal capabilities with external realities to generate actionable strategies. Provide real-world examples for the SO, ST, WO, and WT quadrants.
  4. Contrast Red Ocean Strategy with Blue Ocean Strategy. Using the ERRC Four Actions Framework, analyze a real-world case study (like Cirque du Soleil, Nintendo, or Yellow Tail) to explain how uncontested market space is created.

Summary

This unit explored the critical forward-looking function of planning and strategic formulation. It detailed the hierarchy of plans, distinguishing between standing plans (policies, rules) and single-use plans (programs, budgets). We examined the paradigm shift from traditional goal setting to Peter Drucker's collaborative Management by Objectives (MBO). The unit then transitioned to advanced strategic frameworks, defining Strategic Intent as a firm's long-term, resource-stretching obsession with winning. We saw how the TOWS Matrix acts as a powerful strategy-generation tool by forcing managers to cross-reference internal factors (strengths/weaknesses) with external factors (opportunities/threats) to create actionable SO, ST, WO, and WT strategies. Finally, the unit challenged traditional competitive models via the Blue Ocean Strategy, demonstrating how companies can use the ERRC grid (Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, Create) to achieve Value Innovation—breaking the value-cost trade-off to make the competition irrelevant and open up entirely new market spaces.
