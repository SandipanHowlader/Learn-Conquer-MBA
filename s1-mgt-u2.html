0. Introduction to Planning

0.1 Meaning and Definition of Planning

Planning is the primary and most fundamental function of management. It is the conscious, systematic process of deciding in advance what is to be done, how it is to be done, when it is to be done, and who is to do it, so that the gap between where an organization stands today and where it wants to be tomorrow is bridged in the most efficient way.

Planning is essentially a decision-making activity — it involves selecting the best course of action from among several alternatives, in the light of the objectives to be achieved.

Koontz and O'Donnell: Planning is deciding in advance what to do, how to do it, when to do it, and who is to do it.

Henri Fayol: Planning is one of the elements of management concerned with "prevoyance" — examining the future and drawing up a plan of action.

George R. Terry: Planning is the selection and relating of facts, and the making and use of assumptions regarding the future, in the visualization and formulation of proposed activities believed necessary to achieve desired results.

0.2 Nature / Characteristics of Planning

  • Goal-oriented — every plan spells out the organization's objectives and the steps needed to reach them.
  • Primary function — planning precedes all other management functions; organizing, staffing, directing and controlling all follow from a plan.
  • Pervasive — planning is done at every level of management, though its scope and detail vary by level.
  • Continuous process — plans are never final; they are reviewed and revised as circumstances change.
  • Futuristic — planning always looks ahead and involves forecasting.
  • Involves choice/decision-making — planning is meaningless unless there are alternatives to choose from.
  • Intellectual process — it requires imagination, foresight and sound judgement rather than guesswork.
  • Flexible — a good plan should be capable of adjustment to unforeseen changes without losing its basic character.

0.3 Steps in the Planning Process

  1. Being aware of opportunities (in the market, competition, customer needs).
  2. Setting objectives / goals for the enterprise and each subordinate unit.
  3. Developing planning premises — the assumptions about the future environment in which the plan will operate.
  4. Identifying alternative courses of action.
  5. Evaluating the alternatives in the light of the goals sought.
  6. Selecting the best alternative (the actual point of decision-making).
  7. Formulating derivative/supporting plans (budgets, schedules, sub-plans).
  8. Numbering the plan by converting it into a budget (quantifying the plan in figures).

0.4 Importance of Planning

  • Provides direction and reduces uncertainty by anticipating change.
  • Minimizes wasteful and overlapping activities by coordinating effort in advance.
  • Sets the standards that make effective control possible — without a plan there is nothing to control against.
  • Encourages innovation and creative thinking about the future.
  • Facilitates decision-making by narrowing choices in advance.

1. Types of Plans

Plans exist in a hierarchy, ranging from very broad, long-term statements of purpose down to highly specific, short-term numerical statements. The eight major types recognized in management literature are Missions, Objectives, Strategies, Policies, Procedures, Rules, Programs and Budgets.

Fig. 1 — Plans narrow in scope as they move from the broad mission at the top toward the highly specific, numerical budget at the bottom; each level operationalizes the one above it.

1.1 Mission

The mission (or purpose) is the fundamental reason for an organization's existence — what it does, for whom, and why. It identifies the basic function or task of an enterprise in society: for example, the mission of a business enterprise is generally the production and distribution of goods and services, while a university's mission is teaching, research and community service.

1.2 Objectives (Goals)

Objectives are the specific end points toward which all managerial activity — planning, organizing, staffing, directing and controlling — is aimed. They define what is to be accomplished, by when, and are often expressed in measurable terms (e.g., "increase market share by 5% in the next fiscal year").

1.3 Strategies

A strategy is the determination of the basic long-term objectives of an enterprise, together with the adoption of courses of action and the allocation of resources necessary to achieve those objectives. It answers the question, "How do we get from where we are to where we want to be?"

1.4 Policies

Policies are general statements or understandings that guide managerial thinking and decision-making in recurring situations. They channel decisions along a particular line without dictating exact action, giving subordinates room for judgement within defined limits (e.g., "we promote from within wherever possible").

1.5 Procedures

Procedures are plans that establish a required, chronological sequence of actions for handling recurring activities. Unlike policies, which guide thinking, procedures prescribe the exact manner in which a task must be carried out (e.g., the step-by-step process for processing a purchase order).

1.6 Rules

Rules spell out specific, required actions or non-actions, leaving no room for discretion (e.g., "no smoking in the factory premises"). A rule may or may not be part of a procedure; it is the simplest type of plan.

1.7 Programs

Programs are a complex set of goals, policies, procedures, rules, task assignments, resources to be employed, and other elements necessary to carry out a given course of action (e.g., a company's annual "Employee Wellness Program" or a "New Product Launch Program").

1.8 Budgets

A budget is a statement of expected results expressed in numerical terms — a "numberized" plan. Because it converts a plan into figures (rupees, hours, units of product), it becomes both a planning tool and a fundamental control device.

2. Hierarchy of Objectives

Objectives in an organization form a hierarchy — a "means-end chain" — running from the broad overall purpose of the enterprise down to the specific objective of each individual employee. The achievement of lower-level objectives is the means by which higher-level objectives are achieved.

  • Overall Purpose / Mission — the broadest statement of why the organization exists.
  • Overall Organizational Objectives (long-term / strategic) — set by top management, covering the whole enterprise.
  • Divisional / Departmental Objectives — set by middle management to support the organizational objectives.
  • Individual Objectives (goals and targets) — set for each employee, so that everyone's daily work contributes upward through the chain.

This chain ensures that a clerk's daily target, a department's quarterly target, and the company's five-year target are all logically linked — achieving the smaller, near-term objective is the means to achieving the larger, longer-term one.

3. Management by Objectives (MBO)

3.1 Concept and Definition

Management by Objectives (MBO) is a comprehensive managerial system that integrates many key management activities in a systematic manner, consciously directed toward the effective and efficient achievement of organizational and individual objectives. The term was popularized by Peter F. Drucker in his 1954 book The Practice of Management, and later developed further by George Odiorne.

Peter Drucker: MBO is a process whereby the superior and subordinate managers of an organization jointly identify its common goals, define each individual's major areas of responsibility in terms of results expected, and use these measures as guides for operating the unit and assessing the contribution of each of its members.

3.2 Process of MBO

Fig. 2 — MBO is not a one-time goal-setting exercise but a repeating cycle in which appraisal results feed directly into the next round of objective setting.

3.3 Key Features of MBO

  • Objectives are set jointly by superior and subordinate, not imposed unilaterally.
  • Objectives are specific, time-bound and, wherever possible, quantified.
  • Emphasis is on results/output rather than on activities or personal traits.
  • Provides for periodic review of progress and self-control by the individual.
  • Performance appraisal is based on actual achievement against agreed objectives.

3.4 Benefits of MBO

  • Improves the quality of planning by forcing managers to set clear, verifiable targets.
  • Clarifies organizational roles and structure, since objectives cascade down the hierarchy.
  • Encourages employee commitment through participation in goal-setting.
  • Provides objective standards for performance appraisal, reducing bias.
  • Improves communication between superiors and subordinates.

3.5 Limitations of MBO

  • Time-consuming; setting and reviewing objectives for every individual takes considerable managerial time.
  • Overemphasis on quantifiable objectives may lead to the neglect of qualitative but important goals.
  • Can encourage a rigid, short-term focus at the expense of long-term thinking.
  • Requires genuine top-management commitment and a participative culture to succeed; imposed "pseudo-MBO" often fails.

4. Verifiable and Non-verifiable Objectives

4.1 Verifiable Objectives

A verifiable objective is one that is stated clearly enough, and usually in measurable/quantifiable terms, that at the end of the period a manager and a subordinate can both objectively determine whether or not it was achieved. Example: "reduce customer complaints by 20% within six months."

4.2 Non-verifiable Objectives

A non-verifiable objective is stated in vague, general, or qualitative terms that cannot be objectively measured, making it difficult to judge with certainty whether it has been achieved. Example: "improve employee morale" or "become a more customer-friendly organization."

Good MBO practice pushes managers to convert as many non-verifiable objectives as possible into verifiable ones — e.g., "improve employee morale" could be restated as "increase the employee engagement survey score from 68% to 80% within the year."

6. Nature and Purpose of Strategies and Policies

6.1 Nature of Strategy

  • Deals with the fundamental, long-term direction of the enterprise.
  • Involves the allocation of significant resources (capital, people, time) toward chosen objectives.
  • Is formulated primarily by top management, since it affects the whole organization.
  • Requires analysis of both the external environment (opportunities/threats) and internal capability (strengths/weaknesses).

6.2 Purpose of Strategy

Strategy exists to give the enterprise a unified sense of direction, to guide the allocation of scarce resources to the areas of greatest opportunity, to create a sustainable competitive advantage over rivals, and to provide a framework within which more detailed plans (policies, procedures, programs) can be developed consistently.

6.3 Nature of Policy

  • General in nature; guides thinking without dictating a single course of action.
  • Provides discretion and flexibility for managers to interpret and apply it to specific situations.
  • Derived from and subordinate to strategy — a policy operationalizes a strategic choice into a standing guideline.
  • Relatively stable over time compared to day-to-day operating decisions.

6.4 Purpose of Policy

Policies exist to ensure consistency of decision-making across the organization, to delegate decision authority without losing overall direction, to save management time by pre-deciding the general approach to recurring situations, and to communicate the organization's values and priorities to employees at every level.

7. Strategic Intent

The concept of strategic intent was introduced by Gary Hamel and C.K. Prahalad in their influential 1989 article in the Harvard Business Review. It refers to an ambitious and compelling long-term vision or goal that stretches an organization far beyond its current resources and capabilities, energizing employees to achieve what might otherwise seem impossible.

7.1 Characteristics of Strategic Intent

  • Sense of direction — provides a clear, long-term competitive target (e.g., "become the global leader" rather than a vague aspiration).
  • Sense of discovery — pushes the organization to explore new competitive territory rather than merely extrapolate the present.
  • Sense of destiny — creates an emotionally compelling goal that employees find worthwhile and motivating.
  • Stability over time — while the tactics to achieve it may change, the intent itself remains fixed over a long horizon.
  • Stretch — deliberately exceeds the organization's current resources, forcing creativity and resourcefulness rather than merely fitting strategy to existing means.

7.2 Importance of Strategic Intent

Strategic intent differs from conventional strategic planning, which typically fits the organization's ambition to its current resources. Strategic intent instead creates an extreme misfit between resources and ambitions deliberately, and challenges the organization to close that gap through innovation, leveraging resources cleverly, and building new capabilities — it is less about the "fit" and more about "stretch and leverage."

8. TOWS Matrix

The TOWS Matrix, developed by Heinz Weihrich, is a conceptual framework for systematically identifying strategic options by matching an organization's internal Strengths and Weaknesses with external Opportunities and Threats. (It is the same underlying analysis as a SWOT analysis, but "TOWS" emphasizes moving from the external environment inward to generate concrete strategy options.)

Fig. 3 — The TOWS Matrix pairs each internal factor (Strengths/Weaknesses) with each external factor (Opportunities/Threats) to generate four distinct families of strategy.

8.1 The Four TOWS Strategy Types

  • SO (Maxi-Maxi): Use internal strengths to take maximum advantage of external opportunities — an aggressive, growth-oriented strategy.
  • WO (Mini-Maxi): Overcome internal weaknesses by exploiting external opportunities — often a turnaround strategy.
  • ST (Maxi-Mini): Use internal strengths to minimize or avoid the impact of external threats — a diversification-type strategy.
  • WT (Mini-Mini): Minimize both internal weaknesses and external threats simultaneously — typically a defensive, retrenchment, or divestment strategy.

9. Blue Ocean Strategy

Blue Ocean Strategy is a concept developed by W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne in their book of the same name (2005). It argues that lasting success comes not from fighting competitors within existing, overcrowded markets ("red oceans"), but from creating new, uncontested market space ("blue oceans") where competition is rendered irrelevant.

9.3 Key Principles of Blue Ocean Strategy

  • Value Innovation — the cornerstone of blue ocean strategy: simultaneously pursuing differentiation and low cost, rather than trading one off against the other.
  • Reconstruct market boundaries — look across alternative industries, strategic groups, buyer chains, and complementary products to find new market space.
  • Focus on the big picture, not the numbers — use visual strategy tools (like the Strategy Canvas) rather than getting lost in data alone.
  • Reach beyond existing demand — target non-customers of the industry, not just existing customers.
  • Get the strategic sequence right — buyer utility, then price, then cost, then adoption.

Summary

This unit examined planning as the primary function of management. It began with the meaning, nature, steps and importance of planning, and then explored the hierarchy of plans — mission, objectives, strategies, policies, procedures, rules, programs and budgets — each narrowing in scope as it moves from broad organizational purpose toward specific numerical detail. The hierarchy of objectives showed how individual, departmental and organizational goals form a connected means-end chain. The unit then covered Management by Objectives (MBO) as a systematic process of joint goal-setting, action planning, self-control and appraisal, along with its benefits and limitations, and distinguished verifiable from non-verifiable objectives and quantitative from qualitative objectives. It examined the nature and purpose of strategies and policies and how they differ, introduced Hamel and Prahalad's concept of strategic intent, and explained two widely used strategic-analysis tools: the TOWS Matrix, which generates SO, WO, ST and WT strategy options by matching internal strengths/weaknesses with external opportunities/threats, and Blue Ocean Strategy, which uses value innovation and the ERRC (Eliminate-Reduce-Raise-Create) framework to help organizations create uncontested market space rather than compete head-on in existing, crowded markets.
