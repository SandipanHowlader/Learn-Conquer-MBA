0. Introduction to Controlling

0.1 Meaning and Definition of Controlling

Controlling is the final function in the management process — the process of measuring actual performance against planned standards, identifying deviations, analyzing their causes, and taking corrective action so that organizational objectives are achieved as planned. Controlling closes the loop that planning opens: without control, there is no way of knowing whether a plan actually worked.

Koontz and O'Donnell: Controlling is the measurement and correction of the performance of activities of subordinates in order to make sure that enterprise objectives and the plans devised to attain them are being accomplished.

Henri Fayol: Control consists of verifying whether everything occurs in conformity with the plan adopted, the instructions issued, and the principles established.

E.F.L. Brech: Control is checking current performance against predetermined standards contained in the plans, with a view to ensuring adequate progress and satisfactory performance.

0.2 Nature and Importance of Controlling

  • Goal-oriented — control has meaning only in relation to the standards derived from the organization's plans and objectives.
  • Forward-looking as well as backward-looking — control is most useful when it prevents future deviations, not merely records past ones.
  • Continuous process — control is exercised at every stage, not just at the end of an activity.
  • Pervasive — every manager, at every level, performs some degree of control.
  • Ensures accomplishment of organizational objectives by keeping performance on track.
  • Facilitates judicious use of resources by promptly highlighting waste or inefficiency.
  • Improves employee motivation by clarifying what is expected and how performance will be judged.
  • Helps cope with uncertainty and change by continually monitoring the environment.

1. Control Process

Controlling is carried out through a logical, sequential process that applies to virtually any activity, from a factory's production line to a firm's overall financial performance.

Fig. 1 — Control is a repeating cycle: the corrective action taken (or the standards themselves) may be revised before the next round of measurement begins.

1.1 The Four Steps Explained

  • Establishing Standards — deriving criteria of performance from the organization's plans and objectives; standards may be quantitative (e.g., cost per unit, sales targets) or qualitative (e.g., customer satisfaction).
  • Measuring Performance — assessing actual performance, ideally in the same units as the standard, so that a meaningful comparison can be made.
  • Comparing Performance with Standards and Identifying Deviations — determining the extent to which actual performance meets, exceeds, or falls short of the standard, and analyzing the significance and cause of any deviation.
  • Taking Corrective Action — where deviations are significant, deciding what remedial steps to take: this could mean changing the way an activity is being performed, or, if the standard itself was unrealistic, revising the standard.

2. Planning-Control Linkage

Planning and controlling are often described as the "Siamese twins" of management — inseparable and mutually dependent. Planning provides the standards against which control measures performance, while control provides the feedback that tells managers whether their plans are working, information which then feeds into the next round of planning.

Fig. 2 — Planning and controlling are two sides of the same coin: each is meaningless without the other.

3. Types of Control

Controls can be classified in several ways, but the most widely used classification, based on when the control is applied relative to the activity, identifies three types.

Fig. 3 — The three types of control differ in timing: before the work begins, while it is in progress, and after it is complete.

3.1 Feedforward (Preliminary / Pre-action) Control

Feedforward control is directed at preventing anticipated problems before they occur, by ensuring that the necessary conditions — quality inputs, skilled staff, adequate resources, clear procedures — are in place before an activity begins. Example: a company inspecting raw materials from suppliers before they enter production, or verifying job candidates' qualifications before hiring.

3.2 Concurrent (Steering / "Yes-No") Control

Concurrent control takes place while an activity is in progress, allowing corrective action to be taken before the activity is completed. Direct supervision is the most common form: a manager observing work as it happens and correcting problems on the spot. Example: a supervisor watching an assembly line and stopping it the moment a defect is spotted.

3.3 Feedback (Post-Action) Control

Feedback control is applied after an activity is completed, comparing actual results with the standard and using the deviation to guide future action. Though it cannot fix the problem in the completed activity, it provides essential, often highly reliable, information for improving future planning and performance. Example: analyzing quarterly financial statements or conducting a post-project review.

4. Characteristics of an Effective Control System

  • Suited to plans and positions — controls should be tailored to the specific plans and to the level and responsibility of the manager using them.
  • Reflective of organizational structure — control information should be routed to the manager responsible for the relevant activity.
  • Economical — the cost of control should not outweigh the benefits it produces.
  • Timely — deviations must be reported quickly enough for corrective action to still be useful.
  • Simple and understandable — an overly complex control system will not be used effectively by those who need it.
  • Flexible — a good system should remain workable even when plans change or unforeseen circumstances arise.
  • Objective — standards and measurements should be as precise and impersonal as possible, minimizing bias.
  • Points up exceptions at critical points — attention should focus on the areas and points most critical to overall performance (the exception principle).
  • Indicates corrective action — an ideal control system does not merely detect deviations, but also suggests remedies.

5. Techniques of Control

Managers use a range of traditional and modern techniques to exercise control over organizational performance.

5.1 Traditional Techniques

Technique Description
Budgetary ControlPreparing budgets (sales, production, cash, capital) that state expected results in numerical terms, then comparing actual results against the budget to detect variances.
Personal ObservationDirect, first-hand supervision of ongoing work — often called "management by walking around."
Statistical Reports and DataAnalysis of data in the form of averages, percentages, ratios and charts to track trends and performance.
Break-Even AnalysisDetermining the volume of sales at which total revenue equals total cost, used to control profitability decisions.
Ratio AnalysisUsing financial ratios (liquidity, profitability, solvency, turnover) to control and evaluate financial performance.
Internal AuditAn independent, ongoing appraisal of financial and operational activities within the organization to ensure compliance and identify weaknesses.
PERT / CPMNetwork-based project planning and control techniques (Program Evaluation and Review Technique / Critical Path Method) used to monitor project schedules and identify critical activities.

5.2 Modern Techniques

Technique Description
Management Information Systems (MIS)Computer-based systems that provide managers with timely, relevant information for planning and control decisions.
Balanced ScorecardA performance-measurement framework that evaluates the organization from four perspectives — financial, customer, internal process, and learning & growth — rather than financial measures alone.
Business AnalyticsUse of data, statistical analysis and predictive/prescriptive models to guide and control decision-making (see Section 6).
BenchmarkingComparing the organization's practices and performance against those of the best-in-class performers to identify improvement opportunities (see Section 7).
Total Quality Management (TQM) toolsStatistical process control charts, Six Sigma, and quality circles used to monitor and continuously improve process quality.

6. Business Analytics

Business analytics is the systematic, data-driven exploration of an organization's information — through statistical analysis, quantitative methods and predictive modelling — to support and improve managerial decision-making, planning and control.

Fig. 4 — As analytics moves from descriptive to predictive to prescriptive, it becomes narrower in scope but delivers progressively greater decision-making value.

6.1 Types of Business Analytics

  • Descriptive Analytics — summarizes historical data to understand what has already happened (e.g., a monthly sales dashboard).
  • Predictive Analytics — uses statistical models and patterns in historical data to forecast what is likely to happen next (e.g., forecasting next quarter's demand).
  • Prescriptive Analytics — goes a step further, using optimization and simulation techniques to recommend specific actions a manager should take (e.g., the optimal inventory level to minimize cost while avoiding stock-outs).

6.2 Role of Business Analytics in Control

Business analytics strengthens every stage of the control process: it helps set more realistic, data-driven standards; enables real-time or near-real-time measurement of performance; automatically flags significant deviations (exception reporting); and, at the prescriptive level, can even recommend the specific corrective action a manager should take — turning control from a purely reactive activity into a proactive, forward-looking one.

7. Benchmarking

Benchmarking is the continuous process of measuring an organization's products, services and practices against those of its toughest competitors or the organizations recognized as industry leaders, in order to identify performance gaps and adopt best practices.

7.1 Types of Benchmarking

  • Internal Benchmarking — comparing performance across different departments, teams or units within the same organization.
  • Competitive Benchmarking — comparing performance directly against known competitors in the same industry.
  • Functional Benchmarking — comparing a specific business function (e.g., logistics, customer service) against the best performers of that function, even in a different industry.
  • Generic Benchmarking — comparing fundamental business processes that are similar regardless of industry (e.g., order processing, billing).

Fig. 5 — Benchmarking is not a one-time comparison; leading organizations repeat the cycle periodically as competitors and best practices keep evolving.

7.2 Benefits of Benchmarking

  • Sets realistic, externally validated performance standards rather than purely internal targets.
  • Identifies specific, actionable best practices rather than vague improvement goals.
  • Builds a culture of continuous improvement and external awareness.
  • Helps prioritize where improvement effort will have the greatest competitive impact.

8. Feed Forward vs. Feedback Control

While Section 3 introduced feedforward, concurrent and feedback control together, feedforward and feedback control are frequently contrasted directly because they represent two fundamentally different philosophies of control — preventing problems in advance versus correcting them after the fact.

Basis Feed Forward Control Feedback Control
TimingApplied before the activity beginsApplied after the activity is completed
OrientationFuture-oriented; prevention-focusedPast-oriented; correction-focused
ObjectiveAnticipate and avoid problems before they occurDetect deviations that have already occurred and learn from them
Ability to fix the current activityCan stop a flawed activity before resources are committedCannot change the completed activity, only guide future ones
ExampleInspecting raw materials before production beginsReviewing last quarter's financial results
Data requirementRequires accurate forecasts and reliable models of the processRequires only accurate historical measurement

In practice, the most effective control systems combine all three types: feedforward control to prevent likely problems, concurrent control to catch problems as they happen, and feedback control to ensure that lessons learned are carried into future cycles of planning.

Summary

This unit examined controlling as the final link in the management process, closing the loop opened by planning. It explained the four-step control process — establishing standards, measuring performance, comparing performance with standards to identify deviations, and taking corrective action — and the close, two-way linkage between planning and control, in which planning supplies the standards and control supplies the feedback for future plans. The unit classified controls by timing into feedforward (preventive, before the activity), concurrent (steering, during the activity) and feedback (corrective, after the activity), and set out the characteristics of an effective control system, including suitability, timeliness, economy, flexibility, objectivity and focus on critical points. It surveyed both traditional techniques of control (budgetary control, personal observation, ratio and break-even analysis, internal audit, PERT/CPM) and modern techniques (MIS, the Balanced Scorecard, business analytics and benchmarking). Business analytics was explained through its three levels — descriptive, predictive and prescriptive — and its role in strengthening every stage of control, while benchmarking was covered as a continuous, cyclical process of comparing the organization against best-in-class performers to close performance gaps. Finally, the unit contrasted feed forward and feedback control directly, showing that the strongest control systems combine both prevention and correction.
