0. Introduction to Decision Making

0.1 Meaning and Definition of Decision Making

Decision making is the process of identifying a problem or opportunity, generating and evaluating possible courses of action, and selecting the alternative that best serves the desired objective. It lies at the heart of every managerial function — a manager plans, organizes, staffs, directs and controls only by continually making decisions.

George R. Terry: Decision-making is the selection, based on some criteria, of one behaviour alternative from two or more possible alternatives.

Peter Drucker: Whatever a manager does, he does through decision-making.

Herbert Simon: Decision-making is essentially synonymous with managing itself.

0.2 The Decision-Making Process

Fig. 1 — Decision-making rarely stops at "select the best option": monitoring the outcome routinely uncovers new problems, restarting the cycle.

0.3 Importance of Decision Making

  • Every managerial function — planning, organizing, staffing, directing and controlling — is put into effect through a chain of decisions.
  • Good decisions help an organization take advantage of opportunities and avoid or minimize threats.
  • Decision-making quality directly affects the efficient use of resources — money, time, people and materials.
  • It is a pervasive activity performed by managers at every level, though the nature of the decisions differs (see Section 5).

1. Rationality in Decision Making

1.1 Concept of Rational Decision Making

A decision is said to be rational if it is logically consistent with the goal being sought and represents the best possible means of achieving that goal, given all the available information. Classical decision theory assumes that a manager acts as a perfectly rational "economic man" who seeks to maximize outcomes.

1.2 Conditions Necessary for Perfect (Ideal) Rationality

  • The decision-maker has a clear, agreed and unambiguous goal to be achieved.
  • All possible alternative courses of action are known.
  • The consequences (costs and benefits) of every alternative are known with certainty.
  • The decision-maker has complete and accurate information, with no time or cost constraints on gathering it.
  • The decision-maker can rank all alternatives and consistently select the one that maximizes the goal.

1.3 Bounded Rationality (Herbert Simon)

In practice, perfect rationality is rarely achievable. Herbert Simon introduced the concept of bounded rationality — the idea that managers operate within the limits of the information available, their own cognitive capacity, and time constraints, and therefore cannot evaluate every possible alternative. As a result, managers often practise "satisficing" — choosing the first alternative that is "good enough" to meet a minimally acceptable threshold, rather than "maximizing" — searching exhaustively for the single best possible alternative.

1.4 Limits to Rationality in Practice

  • Incomplete or imperfect information about alternatives and their consequences.
  • Limited time available to search for and evaluate every alternative.
  • Cost of gathering additional information may exceed its value.
  • Personal biases, values, habits and organizational politics influence the "rational" choice.
  • Complexity of the environment makes it impossible to foresee every consequence.

2. Marginal Analysis

Marginal analysis is a quantitative technique for decision-making that compares the additional (marginal) benefit of a course of action with its additional (marginal) cost, in order to find the point at which resources are being used most efficiently.

Marginal Cost (MC): the addition to total cost caused by producing/undertaking one more unit of output or activity.

Marginal Revenue/Benefit (MR): the addition to total revenue or benefit caused by that one additional unit.

Decision Rule: Keep expanding the activity as long as Marginal Revenue > Marginal Cost. The optimum point is reached where MR = MC — beyond this point, each additional unit costs more than it earns.

Application: A firm deciding how many additional units to produce, how many extra salespeople to hire, or how much additional advertising expenditure to incur can use marginal analysis: it should keep adding resources only as long as the extra revenue/benefit generated exceeds the extra cost incurred.

Limitations: Marginal analysis assumes that costs and revenues at the margin can be measured accurately, which is often difficult in practice, especially for decisions involving qualitative or long-term, hard-to-quantify benefits.

3. Cost-Effectiveness Analysis

Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) is a technique for comparing the relative costs of different courses of action that lead to a similar (often non-monetary) outcome, in order to identify the alternative that achieves the desired result at the lowest cost, or achieves the greatest effect for a given cost.

3.1 Cost-Effectiveness Analysis vs. Cost-Benefit Analysis

Basis Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA) Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA)
Measurement of outputOutput/benefit measured in non-monetary units (e.g., lives saved, students trained, defects reduced)Output/benefit measured in monetary terms
UseBest when the benefit is difficult or inappropriate to value in money termsBest when both costs and benefits can be reasonably expressed in money terms
Typical fieldPublic health, education, defence, social programsInvestment appraisal, capital budgeting
Comparison methodCompares cost per unit of effect across alternativesCompares net monetary benefit (benefit minus cost)

3.2 Steps in Cost-Effectiveness Analysis

  1. Clearly define the objective and the measure of effectiveness (e.g., "number of trainees successfully certified").
  2. Identify all feasible alternative courses of action that could achieve this objective.
  3. Estimate the total cost of each alternative.
  4. Estimate the level of effectiveness each alternative is expected to achieve.
  5. Compute the cost-effectiveness ratio (Cost ÷ Units of Effect) for each alternative.
  6. Select the alternative offering the lowest cost per unit of effect (or highest effect per unit of cost).

Example: If Training Program A costs ₹5,00,000 and certifies 250 employees (₹2,000 per employee), while Program B costs ₹6,00,000 and certifies 400 employees (₹1,500 per employee), Program B is more cost-effective even though its total cost is higher.

4. Selecting an Alternative

Once alternatives have been identified and evaluated, a manager must choose one. Management theory recognizes three broad bases on which such a selection is typically made:

4.1 Selection Based on Experience

Relying on past experience is one of the most common bases for choosing among alternatives — a manager tends to repeat courses of action that succeeded before and avoid those that failed. While experience can be a valuable teacher, over-reliance on it may cause a manager to reject a good new alternative simply because it does not resemble past successes, or to repeat past mistakes if the underlying situation has actually changed.

4.2 Selection Based on Experimentation

Trying out a course of action on a small scale (e.g., a pilot project, a test market) before committing fully is a powerful, though often costly and time-consuming, way of selecting an alternative. It is most useful when the outcome of a decision genuinely cannot be predicted through analysis alone, such as in testing a genuinely new product or process.

4.3 Selection Based on Research and Analysis

The most systematic approach involves researching the relationships among the key variables, constraints and premises that bear upon the goal being sought, often supported by quantitative models, statistical analysis, and techniques like marginal analysis and cost-effectiveness analysis (Sections 2 and 3). This is generally the most reliable technique for major or complex decisions, though it is also the most resource-intensive.

5. Programmed and Non-Programmed Decisions

Herbert Simon classified organizational decisions into two broad types, based on how routine or novel the underlying problem is.

5.1 Programmed Decisions

Programmed decisions are made in response to recurring, structured, routine problems for which a definite procedure has already been worked out. Because the situation occurs repeatedly, the organization develops standing plans (policies, procedures, and rules — see Unit 2) to handle it, so managers need not "reinvent the wheel" each time. Example: approving a routine leave application, or re-ordering stock when inventory falls below a set level.

5.2 Non-Programmed Decisions

Non-programmed decisions deal with novel, unstructured, and unusual problems that have not arisen before in quite the same form, so no ready-made procedure exists. These decisions require creativity, judgement, and often extensive analysis. Example: deciding whether to acquire a competitor, or how to respond to a sudden, unprecedented crisis.

Basis Programmed Decisions Non-Programmed Decisions
Nature of problemRoutine, structured, repetitiveNovel, unstructured, one-of-a-kind
Basis of decisionEstablished policies, procedures and rulesJudgement, creativity, intuition, and careful analysis

6. Decision-Making under Certainty, Risk and Uncertainty

Every decision is made under one of three broad conditions, depending on how much the decision-maker knows about the outcomes that will follow from each alternative.

Fig. 3 — Decision conditions form a spectrum: as available information decreases, a manager moves from certainty to risk to uncertainty.

6.1 Decision-Making under Certainty

Under certainty, the decision-maker has complete and accurate knowledge of each alternative and the exact outcome it will produce. There is only one possible result for each course of action, so the decision reduces to simple comparison and selection of the alternative with the best known outcome. Example: choosing the cheapest of three quoted, guaranteed prices for an identical raw material from three suppliers.

6.2 Decision-Making under Risk

Under risk, the decision-maker does not know for certain which outcome will occur, but can estimate the probability

  • Expected Value (EV) Analysis — for each alternative, multiply each possible outcome by its probability of occurring and sum the results; the alternative with the highest expected value is generally preferred.
  • Decision Trees — a diagrammatic technique that lays out decision points, chance events, probabilities and payoffs in sequence, helping visualize complex, multi-stage decisions.

Fig. 4 — Since EV(A) = ₹56,000 exceeds EV(B) = ₹45,000, a purely expected-value-based decision-maker facing risk would select Alternative A.

6.3 Decision-Making under Uncertainty

Under uncertainty, the decision-maker can identify the possible outcomes but has no reliable basis for assigning probabilities to them — the situation is too new, unstable or complex. Because probabilities are unknown, managers fall back on judgement-based decision criteria, including:

  • Maximax criterion ("optimistic") — identify the best possible outcome for each alternative, then choose the alternative with the best of these best outcomes.
  • Maximin criterion ("pessimistic" / Wald criterion) — identify the worst possible outcome for each alternative, then choose the alternative whose worst outcome is the least bad (i.e., maximize the minimum gain).
  • Minimax Regret criterion (Savage criterion) — for each alternative, compute the maximum "regret" (the gap between what was achieved and the best that could have been achieved under each state of nature), then choose the alternative that minimizes this maximum regret.
  • Laplace criterion (equal probability / principle of insufficient reason) — assume all possible states of nature are equally likely, and choose the alternative with the highest average payoff.

Summary

This unit examined decision-making as the core activity underlying every managerial function. It opened with the meaning of decision-making and the sequential, feedback-driven process by which problems are defined, criteria set, alternatives generated and evaluated, and a choice implemented and monitored. It then discussed the concept of rationality, the conditions required for perfect rationality, and Herbert Simon's idea of bounded rationality and "satisficing" as a realistic description of how managers actually decide. Two quantitative decision techniques were covered: marginal analysis, which compares marginal cost against marginal benefit to find the optimum point of an activity, and cost-effectiveness analysis, which identifies the least-cost way of achieving a given non-monetary objective. The unit explained the three bases for selecting an alternative — experience, experimentation, and research and analysis — and Herbert Simon's classification of decisions into programmed (routine, structured, common at lower levels) and non-programmed (novel, unstructured, common at top levels). Finally, it distinguished decision-making under certainty (outcomes fully known), risk (outcomes known with assignable probabilities, addressed through expected value analysis and decision trees), and uncertainty (probabilities unknown, addressed through the Maximax, Maximin, Minimax Regret and Laplace criteria).
