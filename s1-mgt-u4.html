0. Structure and Process of Organizing

0.1 Meaning and Definition of Organizing

Organizing is the management function that follows planning. It is the process of identifying and grouping the work to be performed, defining and delegating authority and responsibility, and establishing relationships to enable people to work together most effectively in accomplishing objectives. The output of organizing is the organization structure — the formal framework of tasks, authority and reporting relationships within the enterprise.

Koontz and O'Donnell: Organizing involves the establishment of an intentional structure of roles through determination and enumeration of activities required to achieve the goals of an enterprise, grouping these activities, assigning them to a group of employees, delegating authority, and providing for coordination.

Louis Allen: Organizing is the process of identifying and grouping the work to be performed, defining and delegating responsibility and authority, and establishing relationships for the purpose of enabling people to work most effectively together in accomplishing objectives.

0.2 Process of Organizing

Fig. 1 — Organizing moves from breaking total work into tasks, to grouping those tasks into departments, assigning them to people, delegating the authority needed to perform them, and finally tying the pieces together through coordination.

0.3 Formal and Informal Organization

  • Formal Organization — the officially sanctioned structure of roles, authority and responsibility deliberately built by management, typically shown on an organization chart.
  • Informal Organization — the network of personal and social relationships that arises spontaneously among people as they interact, cutting across the formal structure. It can speed up communication and build cohesion, but can also spread rumours or resist formal authority if not well managed.

0.4 Importance of Organizing

  • Facilitates administration by ensuring the smooth working of all managerial functions.
  • Facilitates growth and diversification of the enterprise by providing a scalable structure.
  • Ensures optimum utilization of resources by preventing duplication of effort.
  • Encourages creativity and initiative by clearly defining the scope of each person's authority.
  • Promotes coordination among the various departments and levels of the enterprise.

1. Principles of Organizing

  • Unity of Objective — the organization structure, and every part of it, should reflect and contribute to the enterprise's overall objectives.
  • Specialization / Division of Work — work should be divided among individuals according to their qualifications and capabilities to secure the benefits of specialization.
  • Span of Management — there is a limit to the number of subordinates a manager can effectively supervise; the structure must respect this limit.
  • Scalar Chain (Chain of Command) — there should be a clear, unbroken line of authority running from the top of the organization to the bottom.
  • Delegation of Authority — authority sufficient to discharge assigned responsibility must be delegated to each managerial position.
  • Responsibility-Authority Parity — the authority delegated to a position should be commensurate (equal) with the responsibility assigned to it.
  • Unity of Command — each person should report to, and receive orders from, only one immediate superior.
  • Balance — a reasonable balance must be maintained among the various principles (e.g., between centralization and decentralization, between span of control and levels).
  • Flexibility — the structure should be capable of adjusting to changes in the internal and external environment without major disruption.
  • Continuity — organizing is a continuous process; the structure must be reviewed and adapted periodically to remain effective.

2. Organizational Levels and Span of Management

2.1 Concept of Span of Management (Span of Control)

The span of management refers to the number of subordinates a manager can effectively direct and supervise. There is an inverse relationship between the span of management and the number of levels in an organization: a narrow span produces a "tall" structure with many levels, while a wide span produces a "flat" structure with few levels.

2.2 Factors Determining an Appropriate Span

  • Capability and training of both the manager and the subordinates.
  • Clarity of delegation and how well-defined the duties are.
  • Rate of change in the organization — a fast-changing environment usually calls for a narrower span.
  • Use of objective standards and effective communication techniques.
  • Complexity and similarity of the tasks being supervised.
  • Amount of time the manager can devote to supervision versus other duties.

Fig. 2 — With the same number of employees, a narrow span of management requires several extra layers of supervision, while a wide span keeps the structure flat.

2.3 Narrow Span vs. Wide Span — Trade-offs

Basis Narrow Span (Tall Structure) Wide Span (Flat Structure)
Levels of managementMore levelsFewer levels
SupervisionClose, detailed supervision possibleSupervisors must delegate more, closer supervision difficult
CommunicationSlower — must pass through many levelsFaster — fewer levels to pass through
CostHigher — more managerial salariesLower — fewer managers needed
SuitabilityComplex, non-routine work requiring close guidanceRoutine, well-understood work with capable, well-trained staff

3. Patterns of Departmentalization

Departmentalization is the process of grouping related activities into manageable units (departments) to achieve the objectives of the enterprise. There is no single best pattern; the appropriate basis depends on the organization's size, strategy and environment.

Fig. 3 — Functional departmentalization is the most common basis, especially for small and medium organizations offering a narrow range of products.

Basis Description Typical Use / Example
FunctionalGroups activities by the primary business function performed (production, marketing, finance, HR).Common in small/medium firms with a narrow product line.
ProductGroups activities around each distinct product or product line, each with its own production, marketing etc.Large diversified firms (e.g., separate divisions for different product categories).
Territorial / GeographicGroups activities by geographic area or region.Firms operating across multiple regions or countries (e.g., North Zone, South Zone).
CustomerGroups activities around distinct types or groups of customers.A bank with separate divisions for retail customers and corporate customers.
Process / EquipmentGroups activities around a distinct production process or type of equipment.A textile mill organized by spinning, weaving and dyeing processes.
MatrixCombines two bases (typically functional and project/product) so that an employee reports to two managers simultaneously.Engineering and consulting firms running multiple simultaneous projects.

4. Delegation of Authority

4.1 Meaning and Elements of Delegation

Delegation is the process by which a manager assigns a part of his or her total workload to others, granting them the authority needed to carry it out. Delegation involves three interrelated elements:

  • Responsibility — the obligation of a subordinate to perform the duties assigned.
  • Authority — the right to make decisions, give instructions and command resources needed to carry out the assigned duty.
  • Accountability — the subordinate remains answerable to the superior for the proper discharge of the responsibility; unlike authority, accountability cannot be delegated away.

4.2 Process of Delegation

  1. Determination and assignment of duties/tasks to the subordinate.
  2. Granting of authority sufficient to perform the assigned tasks.
  3. Creation of an obligation (responsibility) on the part of the subordinate to perform.
  4. Establishment of accountability — the subordinate remains answerable to the superior for results.

4.3 Principles of Effective Delegation

  • Parity of authority and responsibility — authority delegated must match the responsibility assigned.
  • Absoluteness of accountability — a superior cannot escape accountability for the acts of subordinates simply by delegating.
  • Unity of command — a subordinate should be answerable to only one superior for a given task.
  • Delegation by results expected — authority delegated should be adequate to ensure the ability to accomplish results expected.

4.4 Barriers to Effective Delegation

  • A superior's reluctance to let go ("I can do it better myself" attitude).
  • Lack of confidence in subordinates' abilities.
  • Fear of being blamed for a subordinate's mistakes.
  • Absence of controls that would give a superior advance warning of problems.
  • Subordinates' own reluctance to accept delegated authority, due to fear of criticism or lack of self-confidence.

5. Decentralization of Authority

5.1 Meaning

Decentralization refers to the systematic delegation of authority throughout the organization, in relation to decisions of importance, from higher to lower levels of management. It is decentralization as a philosophy of organizing, not merely the delegation of a single task — the more decisions and the more important the decisions made at lower levels, the greater the degree of decentralization.

5.2 Centralization vs. Decentralization

Fig. 4 — No organization is ever completely centralized or completely decentralized; the practical question is always one of degree.

5.3 Factors Determining the Degree of Decentralization

  • Cost and significance of the decision — higher-value decisions tend to stay more centralized.
  • Size and geographic spread of the organization — larger, more dispersed organizations tend to decentralize more.
  • Availability of capable, trained managers at lower levels.
  • Desire for uniformity of policy across the organization.
  • Availability of effective controls to monitor decentralized decisions.
  • The pace of change in the business environment — rapidly changing conditions often favour more decentralization for faster local response.

5.4 Delegation vs. Decentralization

Basis Delegation Decentralization
MeaningAssignment of authority by one individual superior to a specific subordinateSystematic dispersal of authority throughout the organization
ScopeA relationship between two peopleAn organization-wide philosophy of structuring authority
NecessityNecessary in every organization, however smallA matter of degree/policy choice; can range from very low to very high
NatureA processThe end result of consistent, extensive delegation across the organization

6. Line, Staff and Functional Authority

Fig. 5 — Line authority runs directly down the chain of command; staff authority is advisory to line positions; functional authority allows a specialist to give direct orders on a specific matter to positions outside their own line of command.

6.1 Line Authority

Line authority is the direct, command-giving relationship in which a superior exercises authority over a subordinate, running in an unbroken line from the top of the organization to the bottom. Line managers (e.g., a Production Manager, Sales Manager) are directly responsible for achieving the organization's basic objectives.

6.2 Staff Authority

Staff authority is the right to advise, recommend or assist line managers, without the power to command them directly. Staff positions (e.g., HR Advisor, Legal Counsel, Management Consultant) exist to support and provide expertise to line positions, but the final decision-making authority remains with the line manager.

6.3 Functional Authority

Functional authority is a special, limited form of line authority given to a staff specialist or a manager in one department, allowing them to directly control specific processes, practices or policies in other departments, in the narrow area of their expertise. For example, a Quality Assurance Head may have functional authority to enforce quality standards directly on the shop floor, even though the shop-floor supervisor otherwise reports through the Production Manager's line.

6.4 Line and Staff Conflict

Because line and staff roles cut across the same organization, friction is common: line managers may resent staff "interference" in operational matters, while staff specialists may feel their expert advice is ignored. This is usually minimized by clearly defining the boundaries of staff authority, ensuring mutual understanding of each other's role, and fostering genuine collaboration rather than rigid turf-protection.

7. Empowerment

7.1 Meaning and Concept

Empowerment goes a step beyond delegation — it means giving employees not just specific tasks and limited authority, but real discretion, resources, and the power to make decisions and take independent action in their area of work, along with a sense of ownership over the outcomes. Empowered employees are trusted to use their own judgement, knowledge and creativity rather than simply following instructions.

7.2 Benefits of Empowerment

  • Faster decision-making, since employees closest to the problem can act without waiting for approval from above.
  • Higher employee motivation, job satisfaction and commitment, as people feel trusted and valued.
  • Improved customer responsiveness, especially in service organizations.
  • Encourages innovation, since employees are free to suggest and try new ideas.
  • Frees senior managers' time for more strategic, higher-value work.

7.3 Requirements for Effective Empowerment

  • Genuine sharing of information, so empowered employees understand the bigger picture.
  • Adequate training and skill development, so employees are actually equipped to make good decisions.
  • A supportive organizational culture that tolerates honest mistakes made in good faith.
  • Clear boundaries — empowerment works within defined limits, not as unlimited freedom.
  • Recognition and reward systems that reinforce empowered behaviour.

8. Coordination — Types and Techniques

8.1 Meaning and Nature of Coordination

Coordination is the orderly synchronization of the efforts of individuals and departments to provide the proper amount, timing and quality of execution, so that unified action is directed toward stated objectives. Mary Parker Follett, a pioneer of coordination theory, identified four key principles: coordination by direct personal contact, coordination in the early stages of planning, coordination as a reciprocal (two-way) relationship among all factors in a situation, and coordination as a continuous process.

8.2 Types of Coordination

  • Internal Coordination — synchronizing the activities of different individuals and departments within the organization.
  • External Coordination — synchronizing the organization's activities with outside parties such as suppliers, customers, government agencies and the community.
  • Vertical Coordination — coordination between different levels of the organizational hierarchy (e.g., between top management and departmental heads).
  • Horizontal Coordination — coordination among people or departments at the same level (e.g., between the marketing and production departments).

8.3 Techniques for Achieving Effective Coordination

  • Clear-cut objectives and well-defined policies that give everyone a common sense of direction.
  • Sound organization structure with clearly defined authority-responsibility relationships, avoiding overlap and confusion.
  • Effective communication — free-flowing, two-way communication across departments and levels.
  • Committees and cross-functional teams that bring representatives of different departments together to jointly resolve issues.
  • Voluntary coordination — encouraging a spirit of cooperation and mutual adjustment among employees rather than relying purely on rules.
  • Liaison personnel / coordinators — individuals or units specifically tasked with linking the work of different departments.
  • Rules, procedures and standard schedules that pre-synchronize routine, interdependent activities.

Summary

This unit examined organizing as the management function that translates plans into a working structure of tasks, authority and relationships. It began with the meaning and process of organizing — dividing work, departmentalizing, assigning duties, delegating authority and establishing coordination — and the key principles that guide sound organization design, including unity of objective, specialization, scalar chain, and unity of command. The unit explained span of management and the trade-off between narrow-span "tall" structures and wide-span "flat" structures, and covered the major patterns of departmentalization — functional, product, territorial, customer, process and matrix. It then discussed delegation of authority (its elements of responsibility, authority and accountability, its process, and common barriers) and decentralization as an organization-wide philosophy of dispersing decision-making authority, distinguishing the two concepts and the factors that determine the appropriate degree of decentralization. The unit explained the differences among line authority (direct command), staff authority (advisory) and functional authority (limited specialist control across departments), and introduced empowerment as an extension of delegation that gives employees genuine discretion and ownership. Finally, it covered coordination — its nature per Mary Parker Follett's principles, its internal/external and vertical/horizontal types, and the practical techniques (clear objectives, sound structure, communication, committees, liaison roles and voluntary cooperation) used to achieve it.
