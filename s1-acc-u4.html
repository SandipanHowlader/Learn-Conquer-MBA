0. Introduction to Financial Statement Analysis

0.1 Meaning and Objectives

Financial statement analysis is the process of critically examining the relationships between different items in a company's financial statements (Unit 3) — the Balance Sheet and Statement of Profit and Loss — to understand the company's profitability, liquidity, solvency and efficiency, and to support informed decision-making by managers, investors and lenders.

  • To assess the profitability and operating efficiency of the business.
  • To assess short-term liquidity and long-term solvency.
  • To evaluate the trend of performance over time and compare it against competitors or industry benchmarks.
  • To assist in forecasting and planning future performance.
  • To support credit, investment and management decisions.

0.2 Tools of Financial Statement Analysis

Fig. 1 — The four techniques covered in this unit each analyze the same underlying financial statements from a different angle.

0.3 Users of Financial Statement Analysis

Management uses it for planning and control; investors and shareholders use it to assess returns and risk; lenders and creditors use it to assess creditworthiness and repayment capacity; and employees, regulators and researchers use it for a variety of assessment and compliance purposes.

0.4 Limitations of Financial Statement Analysis

  • Based on historical data, which may not indicate future performance.
  • Affected by the accounting policies chosen by the company (e.g., inventory valuation or depreciation method — Unit 2), limiting comparability.
  • Ignores qualitative factors such as management quality, brand reputation, and employee morale.
  • Price-level (inflation) changes are usually ignored, since figures are stated at historical cost (Unit 1).

1. Ratio Analysis

1.1 Meaning and Significance

A ratio expresses the mathematical relationship between two related figures drawn from the financial statements. Ratio analysis converts raw, absolute figures into relative, comparable measures, making it possible to compare a company's performance across time periods, against competitors, and against industry norms.

1.2 Classification of Ratios

Fig. 2 — The four major families of ratios together give a rounded view of a company's short-term liquidity, long-term solvency, profitability and operational efficiency.

1.3 Liquidity Ratios

  • Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities = 3,00,000 ÷ 1,00,000 = 3 : 1 — for every ₹1 of current liabilities, the company holds ₹3 of current assets, indicating strong short-term liquidity.
  • Quick (Acid-Test) Ratio = (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities = (3,00,000 − 1,00,000) ÷ 1,00,000 = 2 : 1 — even excluding the least liquid current asset (inventory), the company can comfortably cover its current liabilities.

1.4 Solvency (Leverage) Ratios

  • Debt-Equity Ratio = Long-Term Debt ÷ Shareholders' Equity = 2,00,000 ÷ 6,00,000 = 0.33 : 1 — a low ratio, showing the company relies mostly on equity rather than borrowed funds.
  • Debt to Total Assets Ratio = (Long-Term Debt + Current Liabilities) ÷ Total Assets = 3,00,000 ÷ 9,00,000 = 33.3% — about a third of total assets are financed by outside liabilities.
  • Interest Coverage Ratio = EBIT ÷ Interest = 2,50,000 ÷ 30,000 = 8.3 times — operating profit is more than eight times the interest obligation, indicating a comfortable margin of safety for lenders.

1.5 Profitability Ratios

  • Gross Profit Ratio = Gross Profit ÷ Sales × 100 = 4,00,000 ÷ 10,00,000 × 100 = 40%.
  • Net Profit Ratio = Net Profit ÷ Sales × 100 = 1,50,000 ÷ 10,00,000 × 100 = 15%.
  • Return on Equity (ROE) = Net Profit ÷ Shareholders' Equity × 100 = 1,50,000 ÷ 6,00,000 × 100 = 25%.
  • Return on Assets (ROA) = Net Profit ÷ Total Assets × 100 = 1,50,000 ÷ 9,00,000 × 100 = 16.67%.

1.6 Activity (Efficiency / Turnover) Ratios

  • Inventory Turnover Ratio = COGS ÷ Average Inventory = 6,00,000 ÷ 1,00,000 = 6 times (Inventory Holding Period ≈ 365 ÷ 6 ≈ 61 days).
  • Debtors Turnover Ratio = Net Credit Sales ÷ Average Debtors = 8,00,000 ÷ 1,00,000 = 8 times (Average Collection Period ≈ 365 ÷ 8 ≈ 46 days).
  • Total Asset Turnover Ratio = Sales ÷ Total Assets = 10,00,000 ÷ 9,00,000 = 1.11 times — every ₹1 invested in total assets generates ₹1.11 of sales.

2. DuPont Analysis

2.1 Meaning and Concept

DuPont Analysis, first developed by the DuPont Corporation, breaks down Return on Equity (ROE) — the single most important measure of shareholder return — into three underlying components, revealing exactly which lever (profitability, efficiency, or leverage) is driving a company's ROE, rather than simply reporting the final percentage.

Fig. 3 — The same 25% ROE calculated directly in Section 1.5 is now decomposed to show it is driven by a 15% profit margin, assets being turned over 1.11 times, and moderate financial leverage of 1.5×.

2.2 The Three-Step DuPont Formula

Return on Equity (ROE) = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier

where:
Net Profit Margin = Net Profit ÷ Sales
Asset Turnover = Sales ÷ Total Assets
Equity Multiplier = Total Assets ÷ Shareholders' Equity

Worked Example (continuing Vivan Industries Ltd.): Net Profit Margin = 15% (Section 1.5); Asset Turnover = 1.11× (Section 1.6); Equity Multiplier = Total Assets ÷ Equity = 9,00,000 ÷ 6,00,000 = 1.5×.
ROE = 15% × 1.11 × 1.5 = 25% — exactly matching the ROE calculated directly in Section 1.5, confirming the decomposition.

2.3 Why DuPont Analysis is Useful

Two companies can report the identical 25% ROE for entirely different reasons — one through a high profit margin on modest sales volume, another through thin margins on high sales volume with heavy borrowing. DuPont analysis lets a manager or investor see which of the three levers (margin, turnover, or leverage) is really driving returns, and whether that return is being achieved through healthy operating performance or through added financial risk.

2.4 The Extended (Five-Step) DuPont Model

A more detailed, five-step version further splits Net Profit Margin into the effects of tax burden (Net Profit ÷ Pre-Tax Profit), interest burden (Pre-Tax Profit ÷ EBIT), and operating margin (EBIT ÷ Sales), isolating the precise impact of a company's tax position and interest costs on its overall ROE.

3. Common Size Analysis

3.1 Meaning (Vertical Analysis)

Common size analysis (also called vertical analysis) restates every line item of a financial statement as a percentage of one common base figure — total Sales for the Statement of Profit and Loss, and Total Assets for the Balance Sheet — making it possible to compare companies of very different sizes, or the same company's structure across different years, on a like-for-like basis.

Fig. 4 — Of every ₹100 of sales Vivan Industries earns, ₹60 goes to cost of goods sold, ₹15 to operating expenses, ₹10 to interest and tax combined, leaving ₹15 as net profit.

4. Trend Analysis

4.1 Meaning (Horizontal Analysis)

Trend analysis (also called horizontal analysis) studies the direction of change in financial statement items over a series of accounting periods, by expressing each year's figure as a percentage of the figure in a selected base year (which is set to 100). This reveals growth or decline trends that a single year's figures cannot show.

4.2 Steps in Preparing a Trend Analysis

  1. Select an appropriate base year (usually the earliest year in the series, or a "normal" representative year) and set its value to 100.
  2. Express every subsequent year's figure for the same item as a percentage of the base year: Trend % = (Current Year Figure ÷ Base Year Figure) × 100.
  3. Compute trend percentages for all relevant items (e.g., Sales, Net Profit, Total Assets) across all the years being studied.
  4. Analyze the resulting trend percentages to identify the direction, consistency and rate of growth or decline.

Fig. 5 — Expressed as an index, Vivan Industries' sales have grown steadily from a base of 100 in 2022 to 167 in 2026 — a 67% increase over five years.

Summary

This unit covered four complementary tools of financial statement analysis. Ratio analysis expresses relationships between financial statement items as liquidity ratios (Current, Quick), solvency ratios (Debt-Equity, Interest Coverage), profitability ratios (Gross Profit, Net Profit, ROE, ROA) and activity ratios (Inventory Turnover, Debtors Turnover, Total Asset Turnover), each illustrated with figures from Vivan Industries Ltd. DuPont Analysis decomposed the company's 25% Return on Equity into Net Profit Margin (15%) × Asset Turnover (1.11×) × Equity Multiplier (1.5×), showing which specific driver — profitability, efficiency, or leverage — is generating the overall return. Common size analysis (vertical analysis) restated every item of the Income Statement as a percentage of Sales and every item of the Balance Sheet as a percentage of Total Assets, enabling comparison regardless of company size. Finally, trend analysis (horizontal analysis) indexed a company's Sales figures to a base year of 100, revealing a steady growth trend to 166.7% over five years — illustrating how the same underlying financial statements can be examined from four distinct, mutually reinforcing angles to build a complete picture of a company's financial health.
