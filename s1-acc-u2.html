0. Introduction

Unit 1 introduced the concepts, conventions and standards that underlie accounting. This unit puts those ideas into practice: it traces the sequence of steps — the accounting cycle — through which a business converts raw transaction data into finished financial statements, and then examines four topics central to preparing those statements accurately: the Income Statement, the Balance Sheet, inventory valuation, and depreciation accounting.

1. The Accounting Cycle

1.1 Meaning and Steps

The accounting cycle is the complete, repeating sequence of steps followed in a given accounting period — from the initial recording of a transaction to the preparation of final financial statements and the closing of the books, ready for the next period to begin.

Fig. 1 — The cycle repeats every accounting period: once the books are closed, the next period's transactions start the sequence again.

1.2 Journal

The journal is the "book of original entry" in which every transaction is first recorded, in chronological order, showing the accounts to be debited and credited along with a brief explanation (narration), following the rule that every debit must have an equal and corresponding credit (the Dual Aspect Concept from Unit 1).

Worked Example: Rohan starts a stationery business on 1 April 2025.

(a) He invests ₹2,00,000 cash as capital.
Cash A/c  Dr.  ₹2,00,000
    To Capital A/c     ₹2,00,000
(Being cash brought in by the owner as capital)

(b) He purchases goods worth ₹60,000 on credit from Mehta & Sons.
Purchases A/c  Dr.  ₹60,000
    To Mehta & Sons A/c     ₹60,000
(Being goods purchased on credit)

(c) He sells goods for ₹35,000 cash.
Cash A/c  Dr.  ₹35,000
    To Sales A/c     ₹35,000
(Being goods sold for cash)

1.3 Ledger

The ledger is the "book of final entry" — a set of individual accounts (e.g., Cash Account, Sales Account, a specific debtor's account) to which every journal entry is subsequently posted, so that the net effect of all transactions on each individual account can be seen at a glance.

1.4 Trial Balance

A trial balance is a statement listing the closing balances of all ledger accounts at a given date, with debit balances in one column and credit balances in another. Because of the dual aspect concept, the two column totals should always be equal; if they are not, it signals that an error has occurred somewhere in the recording or posting process. A trial balance is a check on the arithmetical accuracy of the books, not a guarantee that every entry is substantively correct.

Worked Example: Continuing Rohan's stationery business, the ledger balances as on 31 March 2026 are as follows:

Since the debit and credit columns total to the same figure (₹2,60,000), the books are arithmetically in balance and ready for the next steps — adjusting entries and the preparation of financial statements.

1.5 Adjusting Entries

Adjusting entries are made at the end of an accounting period to ensure that revenues and expenses are recorded in the period to which they actually relate (per the Matching and Accrual concepts from Unit 1), even if cash has not yet changed hands. Common adjustments include accrued expenses/income, prepaid expenses/unearned income, and depreciation (Section 4).

1.6 Closing Entries

Closing entries transfer the balances of all temporary (nominal) accounts — revenues and expenses — into the Profit & Loss Account, and ultimately into the Capital account, reducing their balances to zero so that the next accounting period can start afresh. Only permanent (real and personal) accounts — assets, liabilities, and capital — carry a balance forward into the next period, appearing in the post-closing trial balance.

2. Preparation of Financial Statements

The accounting cycle culminates in two central financial statements: the Income Statement, which measures performance (profit or loss) over a period, and the Balance Sheet, which shows financial position at a single point in time.

2.1 Income Statement (Trading and Profit & Loss Account)

Meaning and Purpose: The Income Statement measures the profit or loss earned by a business over an accounting period, by matching the revenue earned against the expenses incurred in earning it. In traditional format, it is prepared in two parts: the Trading Account, which calculates Gross Profit, and the Profit & Loss Account, which calculates Net Profit.

2.2 Balance Sheet

Meaning and Purpose: The Balance Sheet is a statement of the financial position of a business as on a specific date, showing what it owns (Assets) and how those assets are financed — through outsiders' claims (Liabilities) and the owner's claim (Capital) — in accordance with the fundamental accounting equation from Unit 1.

2.3 Classification of Assets and Liabilities

  • Non-Current (Fixed) Assets — assets held for long-term use in the business rather than for resale, such as land, buildings, plant and machinery (shown net of accumulated depreciation — see Section 4).
  • Current Assets — assets expected to be converted into cash, sold, or consumed within one operating cycle/year, such as cash, bank balances, debtors, and closing inventory (see Section 3).
  • Non-Current Liabilities — obligations not due for settlement within the next year, such as long-term bank loans and debentures.
  • Current Liabilities — obligations due for settlement within the next year, such as creditors, short-term loans, and outstanding expenses.

2.4 How the Income Statement and Balance Sheet Are Linked

The two statements are not independent — the Net Profit calculated in the Income Statement (Section 2.1) is added to (or a Net Loss subtracted from) the owner's Capital shown in the Balance Sheet, directly connecting the period's operating performance to the closing financial position. Similarly, the Closing Stock figure used to calculate Gross Profit in the Trading Account (Section 3) is the same figure that appears as inventory under Current Assets in the Balance Sheet.

3. Inventory Valuation

3.1 Meaning and Importance

Inventory (stock) valuation is the process of assigning a monetary value to the goods a business holds unsold at the end of an accounting period. Because closing stock appears in both the Trading Account (reducing Cost of Goods Sold and thereby increasing Gross Profit) and the Balance Sheet (as a Current Asset), the method chosen to value inventory directly affects both reported profit and financial position.

3.2 Methods of Inventory Valuation

Fig. 4 — FIFO and LIFO make different assumptions about the flow of costs, which — especially in periods of changing prices — leads to different reported profit and closing stock values.

  • FIFO (First-In, First-Out) — assumes the earliest (oldest) purchased units are sold first, so closing stock is valued at the most recent purchase prices. In a period of rising prices, FIFO results in a lower Cost of Goods Sold and a higher reported profit.
  • LIFO (Last-In, First-Out) — assumes the most recently purchased units are sold first, so closing stock is valued at older (often lower) prices. In a period of rising prices, LIFO results in a higher Cost of Goods Sold and lower reported profit. LIFO is not permitted under Ind AS or IFRS, though it remains permitted in some jurisdictions (e.g., under US GAAP).
  • Weighted Average Cost Method — values both cost of goods sold and closing stock at the weighted average cost of all units available for sale during the period, smoothing out the effect of price fluctuations.
  • Specific Identification Method — each individual unit of inventory is tracked and valued at its own actual, specific cost; practical mainly for businesses dealing in high-value, easily distinguishable items (e.g., automobiles, jewellery).

Worked Example: A trader's purchase and sale record for a single product during March 2026 is as follows:

Because prices were rising through the month, FIFO — which assumes the oldest, cheapest units were sold first — leaves the most expensive units in closing stock, giving the highest stock value and (through a lower Cost of Goods Sold) the highest reported Gross Profit. LIFO produces the opposite result, and the Weighted Average method falls in between.

3.3 Lower of Cost or Net Realisable Value (LCNRV)

Under the Convention of Conservatism (Unit 1), accounting standards require inventory to be valued at whichever is lower: its historical cost, or its Net Realisable Value (NRV — the estimated selling price less estimated costs to complete and sell). This prevents a business from overstating the value of inventory that has fallen in value or become obsolete or damaged.

4. Accounting for Depreciation

4.1 Meaning and Need

Depreciation is the systematic allocation of the cost of a tangible fixed asset over its useful life, reflecting the gradual reduction in the asset's value as it is used to generate revenue. Charging depreciation follows directly from the Matching Concept (Unit 1) — since a fixed asset helps generate revenue over several years, a fair share of its cost must be charged as an expense in each of those years, not entirely in the year it was purchased.

4.2 Causes of Depreciation

  • Wear and tear from regular use of the asset.
  • Effluxion of time — the mere passage of time can reduce an asset's useful economic life, even without use.
  • Obsolescence — the asset becomes outdated due to new technology or changes in methods/fashion.
  • Depletion — the physical exhaustion of a wasting natural resource (e.g., a mine or oil well).
  • Accidents — sudden physical damage that reduces the asset's usefulness.

4.3 Methods of Depreciation

Fig. 5 — SLM charges an equal amount of depreciation every year, while WDV charges a fixed percentage of the reducing book value, so the annual charge is highest in the earliest years.

Method Basis of Calculation Pattern of Charge Typical Use
Straight Line Method (SLM)(Cost − Residual Value) ÷ Useful Life, i.e. a fixed amount each yearEqual depreciation charge every yearAssets that lose value uniformly (e.g., furniture, buildings)
Written Down Value (WDV) / Diminishing Balance MethodA fixed percentage applied to the reducing book value each yearHigher charge in early years, tapering off laterAssets that lose efficiency/value faster when new (e.g., vehicles, machinery, technology)
Units of Production MethodDepreciation per unit produced/used × actual units produced in the yearVaries directly with actual usageAssets whose wear depends on usage rather than time (e.g., production machinery)
Sum-of-the-Years'-Digits MethodCost (less residual value) × a declining fraction based on remaining useful lifeAccelerated — higher in early years, similar in spirit to WDVAssets expected to be more productive when new

To record depreciation:

To transfer depreciation to Profit & Loss Account:

Worked Example (Year 1, Straight Line Method, from Section 4.3):
Depreciation A/c  Dr.  ₹20,000
    To Machinery A/c     ₹20,000
(Being depreciation charged on machinery for the year)

Profit & Loss A/c  Dr.  ₹20,000
    To Depreciation A/c     ₹20,000
(Being depreciation transferred to the Profit & Loss Account)

In the Balance Sheet, the asset is generally shown at its original cost less accumulated depreciation to date (i.e., at its "net book value" or "written down value"), so that the Balance Sheet reflects the asset's remaining, undepreciated value.

4.5 Factors Affecting the Amount of Depreciation

  • Original cost of the asset, including all costs necessary to bring it to working condition.
  • Estimated useful life of the asset — the period over which it is expected to be used.
  • Estimated residual (scrap) value — the expected value of the asset at the end of its useful life.
  • Method of depreciation adopted, and the consistency with which it is applied (Convention of Consistency, Unit 1).

Summary

This unit traced the accounting cycle — the sequence of identifying transactions, journalizing, posting to the ledger, preparing a trial balance, making adjusting entries, preparing financial statements, passing closing entries, and preparing a post-closing trial balance — that repeats every accounting period. It then examined the preparation of the two principal financial statements: the Income Statement, prepared as a Trading Account (to calculate Gross Profit) followed by a Profit & Loss Account (to calculate Net Profit), and the Balance Sheet, which shows Equity and Liabilities balanced against Assets, classified into current and non-current categories, and linked to the Income Statement through the Net Profit and Closing Stock figures. The unit covered inventory valuation — the FIFO, LIFO, Weighted Average and Specific Identification methods, the Lower of Cost or Net Realisable Value rule, and the effect of over- or under-valuing stock on reported profit and financial position. Finally, it explained accounting for depreciation — its meaning, causes, and the Straight Line, Written Down Value, Units of Production and Sum-of-the-Years'-Digits methods, along with the relevant journal entries and the key factors (cost, useful life, residual value and method) that determine the amount of depreciation charged each year.
