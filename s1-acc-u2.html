1. Accounting Cycle

1.1 Meaning

The Accounting Cycle refers to the complete sequence of accounting procedures that are performed in each accounting period, starting from the identification and recording of a transaction and ending with the preparation of final accounts and the closing of books, ready for the next period[cite: 6].

It is called a "cycle" because the same sequence of steps is repeated in each subsequent accounting period[cite: 6]. Understanding the accounting cycle is fundamental for managers because every figure presented in the Balance Sheet or Income Statement is the end-result of this systematic process — appreciating the cycle helps a manager understand the reliability, timing and limitations of the numbers they use for decision-making[cite: 6].

1.2 Steps in the Accounting Cycle

  • Identifying and Analysing Transactions: Every business event is examined to determine whether it is a financial transaction (i.e., measurable in money) and, if so, which two or more accounts it affects[cite: 6].
  • Recording in the Journal (Journalising): The transaction is recorded in the Journal, the book of original/prime entry, in chronological order, applying the rules of debit and credit[cite: 6].
  • Posting to the Ledger: Journal entries are transferred (posted) to the relevant accounts in the Ledger, the book of final entry that groups all transactions relating to a particular account at one place[cite: 6].
  • Balancing the Ledger Accounts: At the end of the period, each ledger account is totalled and balanced to find its net debit or credit balance[cite: 6].
  • Preparing the Trial Balance: A statement listing all ledger account balances (debit and credit) is prepared to check the arithmetical accuracy of the double-entry records — total debits must equal total credits[cite: 6].
  • Passing Adjusting Entries: Entries are passed for accrued/outstanding expenses, prepaid expenses, accrued/unearned income, depreciation, closing stock, provisions for bad debts, etc., so that revenues and expenses are correctly matched to the period (matching concept)[cite: 6].
  • Preparing the Adjusted Trial Balance (optional worksheet step): Incorporates the effect of adjusting entries before final accounts are drawn up; often done informally through a worksheet[cite: 6].
  • Preparing the Final Accounts / Financial Statements: The Trading Account, Profit and Loss Account (Income Statement) and Balance Sheet are prepared from the (adjusted) trial balance to show the results of operations and the financial position[cite: 6].
  • Passing Closing Entries: All nominal accounts (expenses and revenues) are closed by transferring their balances to the Trading and Profit & Loss Account, so that they start the next period with a zero balance[cite: 6].
  • Preparing the Post-Closing Trial Balance: A trial balance consisting only of balance sheet accounts (assets, liabilities, capital) is prepared to confirm the books are still in balance and ready for the next accounting period[cite: 6].
  • Passing Reversing Entries (optional): Certain adjusting entries (e.g., for accrued expenses/income) may be reversed at the start of the next period to simplify subsequent routine recording[cite: 6].

1.4 Journal, Ledger and Trial Balance — A Quick Recap

(a) Journal: The Journal is the book of original entry in which transactions are first recorded in chronological order, in the form of a journal entry, applying the rule "Debit what comes in / Credit what goes out" (Real Accounts); "Debit the receiver / Credit the giver" (Personal Accounts); and "Debit all expenses and losses / Credit all incomes and gains" (Nominal Accounts)[cite: 6]. Each entry also carries a brief explanation known as a narration[cite: 6].

(b) Ledger: The Ledger is the principal book of accounts, containing all accounts (assets, liabilities, capital, revenues, expenses) in a classified form[cite: 6]. Entries from the journal (and subsidiary books) are posted to the respective ledger accounts, which are periodically balanced to find the net position of each account[cite: 6].

(c) Trial Balance: A Trial Balance is a statement showing the debit and credit balances of all ledger accounts as on a particular date[cite: 6]. Since every transaction affects at least two accounts with equal debit and credit amounts (dual aspect concept), the total of the debit column of the trial balance must equal the total of the credit column, if postings and balancing have been done correctly (Total Debit Balances = Total Credit Balances)[cite: 6]. Note: A tallied trial balance confirms only the arithmetical accuracy of postings — it does not guarantee that no errors exist[cite: 6].

1.5 Adjusting Entries — Common Types

  • Outstanding/Accrued Expenses: Expenses incurred but not yet paid, e.g., outstanding salary — added to the relevant expense and shown as a current liability[cite: 6].
  • Prepaid Expenses: Expenses paid in advance for a future period, e.g., prepaid insurance — deducted from the expense and shown as a current asset[cite: 6].
  • Accrued Income: Income earned but not yet received, e.g., accrued interest — added to income and shown as a current asset[cite: 6].
  • Income Received in Advance (Unearned Income): Income received but not yet earned, e.g., rent received in advance — deducted from income and shown as a current liability[cite: 6].
  • Depreciation: The fall in value of fixed assets due to use, wear and tear or passage of time — charged as an expense and deducted from the asset in the Balance Sheet[cite: 6].
  • Closing Stock: Value of unsold inventory at the end of the period — credited to the Trading Account and shown as a current asset in the Balance Sheet[cite: 6].
  • Bad Debts and Provision for Doubtful Debts: Amounts owed by customers that are irrecoverable, or estimated to be doubtful, are written off/provided for, reducing debtors and profit[cite: 6].
  • Provision for Discount on Debtors/Creditors: An estimated allowance for cash discount likely to be given to debtors or received from creditors on prompt settlement[cite: 6].

1.6 Importance of the Accounting Cycle

  • Ensures that all transactions are recorded systematically and none are omitted[cite: 6].
  • Provides a logical, verifiable trail from source document to final financial statement (an "audit trail")[cite: 6].
  • Helps detect errors through the trial balance before financial statements are finalised[cite: 6].
  • Ensures that financial statements reflect the true profit/loss and financial position, since adjustments correctly match revenues and expenses to the period[cite: 6].
  • Provides a uniform, repeatable process, enabling consistent and comparable financial reporting period after period[cite: 6].

1.9 Errors and Their Rectification

  • Errors of Omission: A transaction is completely omitted from the books, e.g., a credit sale not recorded at all[cite: 6]. Since neither a debit nor a credit is posted, the trial balance still agrees[cite: 6].
  • Errors of Commission: A transaction is recorded but with a wrong amount, in the wrong account of the correct class, or wrongly posted/totalled, e.g., posting ₹5,000 as ₹500, or debiting the wrong debtor's account[cite: 6].
  • Errors of Principle: A transaction is recorded by violating a fundamental accounting principle, e.g., treating a capital expenditure (purchase of machinery) as a revenue expenditure (repairs)[cite: 6].
  • Compensating Errors: Two or more errors that cancel out each other's effect on the trial balance totals, e.g., an overcasting of the Purchases Book by ₹1,000 combined with an undercasting of the Sales Book by ₹1,000[cite: 6].
Disclosed by Trial Balance[cite: 6] Not Disclosed by Trial Balance[cite: 6]
One-sided posting errors (posting only a debit or only a credit)[cite: 6]Errors of complete omission (transaction not recorded at all)[cite: 6]
Errors in totalling of subsidiary books or the trial balance itself[cite: 6]Errors of principle (e.g., capital expenditure treated as revenue expenditure)[cite: 6]
Posting an amount twice to the same account, or an incorrect amount on one side only[cite: 6]Compensating errors (which cancel each other out)[cite: 6]
Omission of an account balance from the trial balance[cite: 6]Recording a transaction in the correct amount but in the wrong account of the same class (e.g., wrong debtor)[cite: 6]

(c) Suspense Account: When the trial balance does not agree and the errors causing the difference cannot be located immediately, a Suspense Account is opened and the difference is posted to it temporarily, so that the final accounts can be prepared without delay[cite: 6]. As and when errors are located, they are rectified through the Suspense Account, which should show a nil balance once all errors are corrected[cite: 6].

2. Preparation of Financial Statements

2.1 Meaning and Objective

Financial statements are the formal, structured reports prepared at the end of an accounting period that summarise the financial performance and position of a business[cite: 6]. The two principal financial statements covered in this unit are the Income Statement (Trading and Profit & Loss Account) and the Balance Sheet[cite: 6].

2.2 The Income Statement (Trading and Profit & Loss Account)

The Income Statement measures the financial performance of a business over a period of time by matching revenues earned against expenses incurred, to arrive at the net profit or net loss for that period[cite: 6].

(a) Trading Account: Prepared first to ascertain the Gross Profit or Gross Loss made on buying and selling of goods[cite: 6]. Gross Profit = Net Sales − Cost of Goods Sold[cite: 6]. Cost of Goods Sold = Opening Stock + Net Purchases + Direct Expenses − Closing Stock[cite: 6].

2.7 Working Capital

An important figure derived from the Balance Sheet, useful to managers for assessing short-term financial health, is Working Capital — the excess of Current Assets over Current Liabilities, representing the funds available for day-to-day operations[cite: 6]. Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities[cite: 6].

3. Inventory Valuation

3.1 Meaning and Importance

Inventory (stock) refers to goods held by a business for the purpose of resale (in a trading concern) or for use in production (raw materials, work-in-progress, finished goods, in a manufacturing concern)[cite: 6]. The value assigned to closing stock directly affects both the Income Statement (through Cost of Goods Sold and Gross Profit) and the Balance Sheet (as a current asset)[cite: 6].

3.2 The Basic Valuation Principle

As per the convention of conservatism and Accounting Standard AS 2 (Valuation of Inventories) / Ind AS 2, inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV), so that unrealised profits are not anticipated and inventories are not overstated[cite: 6].

3.3 Components of Cost of Inventory

  • Cost of Purchase: Purchase price, plus import duties and other non-refundable taxes, plus freight, carriage inwards and other costs directly attributable to acquisition, less trade discounts and rebates[cite: 6].
  • Cost of Conversion: Costs directly related to units of production, such as direct labour, plus a systematic allocation of fixed and variable production overheads[cite: 6].
  • Other Costs: Only those costs incurred in bringing the inventory to its present location and condition, e.g., specific design costs for a customer's order[cite: 6].
  • Costs excluded from inventory: Abnormal wastage, storage costs (unless necessary for production), administrative overheads unrelated to production, and selling and distribution costs — all of these are expensed, not included in inventory cost[cite: 6].

3.4 Methods of Inventory Valuation

  • (a) First-In-First-Out (FIFO) Method: It is assumed that the goods purchased first are sold first; therefore, the closing stock is valued at the cost of the most recently purchased units[cite: 6]. During periods of rising prices, FIFO results in a lower cost of goods sold and consequently a higher reported profit[cite: 6].
  • (b) Last-In-First-Out (LIFO) Method: It is assumed that the goods purchased last are sold first; therefore, the closing stock is valued at the cost of the earliest purchased units[cite: 6]. Not permitted under current Indian and international accounting standards for external financial reporting[cite: 6].
  • (c) Weighted Average Cost Method: The cost of each unit is determined from the weighted average of the cost of similar items at the beginning of the period and the cost of similar items purchased during the period[cite: 6]. Smoothens out the effect of price fluctuations[cite: 6].

3.7 Periodic vs Perpetual Inventory Systems

Periodic Inventory System: Inventory records are updated only at the end of the accounting period, based on a physical stock count; the Cost of Goods Sold is derived as a balancing figure (Opening Stock + Purchases − Closing Stock)[cite: 6].

Perpetual Inventory System: A continuous, running record of each inventory item is maintained through stores ledger/bin cards, updated after every purchase and sale, so that the balance of stock is known at any time without a physical count[cite: 6].

3.8 Inventory Turnover Ratio

Inventory Turnover Ratio = Cost of Goods Sold ÷ Average Inventory[cite: 6]. Average Inventory = (Opening Stock + Closing Stock) ÷ 2[cite: 6].

4. Accounting for Depreciation

4.1 Meaning and Definition

Depreciation is the systematic allocation of the depreciable cost of a fixed (tangible) asset over its useful life, representing the gradual, permanent and continuing reduction in the value of the asset due to use, wear and tear, effluxion of time, or obsolescence[cite: 6]. As per AS 6 / Ind AS 16, depreciation is an expense that must be charged to the Profit & Loss Account each year the asset is used, in accordance with the matching concept[cite: 6].

4.2 Causes of Depreciation

  • Wear and Tear: Physical deterioration of an asset due to its regular use in business operations[cite: 6].
  • Efflux of Time: Certain assets (e.g., leasehold property, patents) lose value merely with the passage of time, regardless of use[cite: 6].
  • Obsolescence: An asset becomes outdated due to new inventions, technological advancement, or changes in fashion/demand, even if it is still physically usable[cite: 6].
  • Depletion: Reduction in the physical quantity of a wasting asset, such as a mine or oil well, as it is extracted/used up[cite: 6].

4.3 Need for Providing Depreciation

  • To ascertain true profit or loss: By matching the cost of using an asset against the revenue it helps generate in each period[cite: 6].
  • To show the true financial position: The Balance Sheet should reflect the asset at its reasonable written-down value, not its original cost, once it has been in use[cite: 6].
  • To provide funds for replacement: Charging depreciation retains profits within the business (since depreciation is a non-cash expense) that can eventually be used towards replacing the asset[cite: 6].
  • To comply with legal requirements: The Companies Act, 2013 and Income Tax Act require depreciation to be charged/allowed in a prescribed manner[cite: 6].

4.5 Methods of Providing Depreciation

  • (a) Straight Line Method (SLM) / Fixed Instalment Method: A fixed and equal amount of depreciation is charged every year over the useful life of the asset, calculated on the original cost[cite: 6]. Annual Depreciation = (Cost of Asset − Estimated Residual Value) ÷ Estimated Useful Life (in years)[cite: 6].
  • (b) Written Down Value (WDV) Method / Diminishing Balance Method: Depreciation is charged at a fixed percentage each year, but the percentage is applied to the reducing book value (original cost less depreciation already charged) rather than to the original cost[cite: 6].

4.10 Profit or Loss on Sale/Disposal of an Asset

When a depreciable asset is sold, discarded, or exchanged, the difference between its sale proceeds (if any) and its book value (written down value) at the time of disposal is recognised as a profit or loss on disposal, transferred to the Profit and Loss Account[cite: 6]. Profit/(Loss) on Sale = Sale Proceeds − Book Value at the Date of Sale[cite: 6].
