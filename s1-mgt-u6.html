0. Leading as a Management Function

Leading (or Directing) is the management function concerned with influencing, motivating and guiding people so that they willingly and enthusiastically contribute toward the achievement of organizational objectives. It is the "people" function of management, built around leadership, motivation and communication — the three themes covered in this unit.

0.1 Leadership vs. Management

Basis Management Leadership
FocusPlanning, organizing, coordinating and controlling resourcesInspiring, influencing and guiding people
Source of influenceFormal position/authorityPersonal qualities, vision and ability to inspire; may or may not come from formal position
OrientationStability, order, efficiencyChange, vision, growth
ScopeA wider function that includes leadership as one elementA narrower, though critical, element within the broader task of management

Every manager is not necessarily a good leader, and every leader is not necessarily a formally appointed manager — but effective managers generally need strong leadership skills to get the best out of their people.

1. Leadership — Definition, Ingredients and Principles

1.1 Meaning and Definition of Leadership

Koontz and O'Donnell: Leadership is the art or process of influencing people so that they will strive willingly and enthusiastically toward the achievement of group goals.

George R. Terry: Leadership is the activity of influencing people to strive willingly for group objectives.

Peter Drucker: Leadership is the lifting of a person's vision to higher sights, the raising of performance to a higher standard, and the building of personality beyond its normal limitations.

1.2 Ingredients of Leadership

Fig. 1 — Koontz and O'Donnell identified four essential ingredients that combine to make leadership effective.

1.3 Principles of Leadership

  • Know yourself and seek self-improvement — understand your own strengths and weaknesses and work to develop them.
  • Be technically and professionally proficient — know your job well enough to guide and support your team.
  • Know your people and look out for their welfare — build genuine understanding of individual team members.
  • Keep people informed — communicate openly and honestly about goals, expectations and changes.
  • Set the example — a leader's own behaviour is the most powerful influence on followers.
  • Ensure tasks are understood, supervised and accomplished — clarity of direction and follow-through.
  • Make sound and timely decisions — decisiveness builds confidence among followers.
  • Develop a sense of responsibility among subordinates — delegate and empower rather than micromanage.
  • Build the team and employ it according to its capabilities — leverage individual strengths for collective results.

2. Trait Theory of Leadership

The trait approach, one of the earliest attempts to explain leadership, holds that effective leaders possess a distinctive set of personal qualities or characteristics that distinguish them from non-leaders.

2.1 Commonly Identified Leadership Traits

  • Intelligence — sound judgement and reasoning ability.
  • Self-confidence — certainty about one's own competence and decisions.
  • Determination / Drive — a strong desire to get the job done, including initiative, persistence and achievement orientation.
  • Integrity — honesty and trustworthiness; the quality that builds followers' confidence.
  • Sociability — the ability to seek out and maintain pleasant social relationships.
  • Emotional stability — the capacity to manage stress and remain composed under pressure.

2.2 Limitations of the Trait Theory

  • No consistent set of traits has been found to guarantee leadership success across all situations.
  • Ignores the influence of the situation and the needs of followers.
  • Difficult to measure traits objectively, and many successful leaders lack one or more "required" traits.
  • Traits alone do not explain leadership behaviour or effectiveness — this limitation gave rise to the behavioural (style-based) theories that followed, including leadership styles and the Managerial Grid.

3. Leadership Styles

Leadership styles describe the recurring pattern of behaviour a leader uses while directing and influencing followers. The classic classification, based on the degree to which a leader shares decision-making authority with the group, identifies three styles.

Fig. 2 — As leadership style moves along the continuum, decision-making authority progressively shifts from the leader alone to the group.

3.1 Autocratic (Authoritarian) Style

The leader makes decisions unilaterally, closely directs subordinates' work, and expects strict obedience without seeking their input. Useful in crises requiring quick decisions or with inexperienced teams, but can lower morale and stifle initiative over time.

3.2 Democratic (Participative) Style

The leader involves subordinates in the decision-making process, encourages open discussion, and values their input before reaching a final decision (which the leader still typically makes). This style tends to build commitment, morale and creativity, though it can be slower in urgent situations.

3.3 Laissez-Faire (Free-Rein) Style

The leader gives subordinates almost complete freedom to set their own goals and work out their own methods, providing resources and information only when asked. It can work well with highly skilled, self-motivated professionals, but risks a lack of direction and coordination with less experienced teams.

4. Managerial Grid (Blake and Mouton)

The Managerial Grid, developed by Robert Blake and Jane Mouton, is a behavioural framework that plots leadership style on two independent dimensions — a leader's concern for production (results, tasks) and concern for people (relationships) — each rated on a scale of 1 (low) to 9 (high), producing 81 possible combinations, of which five are commonly highlighted.

Fig. 3 — (9,9) Team Management, which combines high concern for both people and production, is considered the most effective managerial style on the grid.

Grid Position Style Name Description
(1,1)Impoverished ManagementMinimum effort on both production and people — merely enough to keep one's job.
(1,9)Country Club ManagementHigh concern for people, low concern for production — a comfortable, friendly atmosphere but weak results.
(9,1)Authority-Obedience (Task Management)High concern for production, low concern for people — efficiency achieved by minimizing human interference.
(5,5)Middle-of-the-Road ManagementBalances adequate work performance with reasonable morale, without excelling at either.
(9,9)Team ManagementHigh concern for both production and people — commitment and results achieved through trust, respect, and shared goals; considered the ideal style.

5. Motivation — Concept

5.1 Meaning and Definition

Motivation refers to the internal and external factors that stimulate desire and energy in people to be continually interested in, and committed to, a job, role or subject, or to make an effort to attain a goal. In a managerial context, it is the process of inspiring people to willingly exert their best effort toward organizational goals.

Fred Luthans: Motivation is a process that starts with a physiological or psychological deficiency or need that activates behaviour or a drive that is aimed at a goal or incentive.

5.2 Importance of Motivation

  • Improves the level of efficiency and productivity of employees.
  • Leads to the optimum utilization of resources, especially human resources.
  • Helps achieve organizational goals by aligning individual and organizational interests.
  • Builds a friendly, cooperative and stable workforce, reducing turnover and absenteeism.

6. Theories of Motivation

6.1 Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

Abraham Maslow proposed that human needs exist in a hierarchy of five levels, and that a satisfied need no longer motivates behaviour — as each lower-level need is largely satisfied, the next higher need becomes the dominant motivator.

Fig. 4 — Once a lower-level need such as physiological or safety needs is reasonably satisfied, it ceases to motivate, and the next higher-level need takes over.

  • Physiological Needs — food, water, shelter, sleep — the most basic needs for survival.
  • Safety Needs — job security, physical safety, financial security, protection from harm.
  • Social / Belongingness Needs — friendship, teamwork, acceptance, love.
  • Esteem Needs — self-respect, recognition, status, achievement.
  • Self-Actualization Needs — realizing one's full potential, personal growth, and creative fulfilment.

6.2 Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory

Frederick Herzberg found that the factors causing job satisfaction are different from those causing job dissatisfaction, and that they operate on two separate continua rather than one single scale.

Fig. 5 — Herzberg's key insight: the opposite of "satisfaction" is not "dissatisfaction" but simply "no satisfaction" — the two are driven by entirely different sets of factors.

  • Hygiene Factors (dissatisfiers) — company policy, salary, working conditions, supervision, job security. Their absence causes dissatisfaction, but their presence alone does not motivate.
  • Motivators (satisfiers) — achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement, the nature of the work itself. Their presence creates genuine job satisfaction and motivation.

6.3 McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y

Basis Theory X Theory Y
Assumption about workersInherently lazy, dislike work, avoid responsibility, must be closely controlledWorkers find work natural and satisfying, seek responsibility, and are capable of self-direction
Management style impliedDirective, controlling, authoritarianParticipative, empowering, supportive
Motivation approachCoercion, close supervision, threats of punishmentInvolvement, recognition, opportunities for growth

6.4 McClelland's Need Theory

David McClelland proposed that individuals are primarily driven by three learned needs, present in varying degrees in every person:

  • Need for Achievement (nAch) — the drive to excel, achieve in relation to a set of standards, and strive to succeed.
  • Need for Power (nPow) — the desire to influence, control, or have an impact on others' behaviour.
  • Need for Affiliation (nAff) — the desire for close, friendly interpersonal relationships.

6.5 Vroom's Expectancy Theory

Victor Vroom proposed that motivation is a function of three factors, multiplied together: Motivation = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence.

  • Expectancy — the belief that increased effort will lead to improved performance.
  • Instrumentality — the belief that improved performance will lead to a specific outcome or reward.
  • Valence — the value or attractiveness the individual places on the expected outcome/reward.

Because these factors are multiplied, if any one of them is zero (e.g., the reward is not valued at all), overall motivation collapses to zero, regardless of the other two factors.

7. Communication — Purpose and Process

7.1 Meaning and Definition

Communication is the process of transmitting information, ideas, emotions and understanding from one person (or group) to another, in a manner intended to produce a response or shared understanding. It is the thread that ties together all managerial functions and levels of the organization.

7.2 Purpose of Communication

  • To inform — sharing facts, data and instructions needed to perform work.
  • To persuade or influence — building agreement or support for a decision or course of action.
  • To coordinate — synchronizing the activities of different people and departments.
  • To motivate — encouraging, recognizing and inspiring employees.
  • To provide feedback — enabling both superiors and subordinates to know how they are doing.
  • To build relationships — fostering trust, understanding and a sense of belonging.

7.3 The Communication Process

Fig. 6 — Communication is complete only when feedback confirms the message was received and understood as intended; "noise" at any stage can distort the message.

  • Sender — the person or source originating the message.
  • Encoding — converting the idea into words, symbols, or gestures that can be transmitted.
  • Message and Channel — the content itself, sent through a chosen medium (spoken, written, digital, visual).
  • Decoding — the receiver interpreting and making sense of the message.
  • Receiver — the person for whom the message is intended.
  • Feedback — the receiver's response, which tells the sender whether the message was understood as intended.
  • Noise — any interference (physical, semantic, or psychological) that distorts the message at any stage of the process.

8. Written, Oral and Non-Verbal Communication

8.1 Written Communication

Written communication uses letters, memos, reports, emails, manuals and notices to convey a message in permanent, recorded form.

AdvantagesLimitations
Provides a permanent record; precise and unambiguous; suitable for complex or lengthy information; can be referred to later. Time-consuming to prepare; lacks immediate feedback; can be impersonal; may be misunderstood without tone or context.

8.2 Oral Communication

Oral communication uses spoken words — face-to-face conversation, telephone calls, meetings, video calls, and presentations — to convey a message.

AdvantagesLimitations
Fast, allows immediate feedback and clarification; conveys tone and emotion; builds personal rapport. No permanent record (unless recorded); can be forgotten or misquoted; less suitable for lengthy or highly technical information.

8.3 Non-Verbal Communication

Non-verbal communication conveys meaning without words, often reinforcing or contradicting the spoken/written message. Key categories include:

  • Kinesics — body language: facial expressions, gestures, posture, eye contact.
  • Proxemics — use of physical space and distance between communicators.
  • Paralanguage — tone, pitch, volume, pace and other vocal qualities that accompany spoken words.
  • Chronemics — the use of time (e.g., punctuality, response time) as a communicative signal.
  • Appearance and artifacts — dress, grooming, and physical surroundings, which also convey messages.

9. Barriers in Communication

  • Semantic Barriers — arise from the way words, symbols or language are used or interpreted differently (jargon, ambiguity, poor translation, badly expressed messages).
  • Psychological Barriers — arise from emotional or mental factors such as premature evaluation, distrust, poor listening, and selective perception.
  • Organizational Barriers — arise from the structure and policies of the organization, such as long scalar chains, information overload, and poorly defined communication channels.
  • Personal Barriers — arise from an individual's own attitudes, such as a superior's fear of losing authority, or a subordinate's fear of criticism, which discourage open communication.
  • Physical/Mechanical Barriers — environmental noise, poor telephone/internet connections, distance, and faulty equipment.

9.1 Overcoming Communication Barriers

  • Clarify ideas before communicating and use simple, unambiguous language.
  • Practise active listening and encourage two-way feedback.
  • Choose the appropriate medium/channel for the message.
  • Be sensitive to the receiver's perspective and emotional state.
  • Follow up to confirm the message was understood as intended.

10. Formal and Informal Communication

10.1 Formal Communication

Formal communication follows the official chain of command and predefined channels laid down by the organization structure. It may flow:

  • Downward — from superiors to subordinates (instructions, policies, directives).
  • Upward — from subordinates to superiors (reports, suggestions, grievances).
  • Horizontal (Lateral) — between people or departments at the same level, for coordination.
  • Diagonal — between people at different levels and different departments, cutting across the usual chain of command.

10.2 Informal Communication (The Grapevine)

Informal communication arises spontaneously out of the social relationships among people, cutting across formal reporting lines. It is often referred to as the "grapevine" because, like a vine, it spreads unpredictably in every direction. It can transmit information faster than formal channels and builds social cohesion, but is also prone to distortion, rumour, and incomplete information.

10.3 Formal vs. Informal Communication

Basis Formal Communication Informal Communication
OriginDeliberately designed by the organizationArises spontaneously from social interaction
Path followedFollows the official chain of commandFollows no fixed path; spreads unpredictably ("grapevine")
SpeedRelatively slower, especially in tall structuresOften much faster
ReliabilityGenerally more accurate and verifiableProne to distortion, exaggeration and rumour

Summary

This unit examined leading and communication — the "people" dimension of management. It began by distinguishing leadership from management and defining leadership through the ingredients (power to influence, understanding people, ability to inspire, and style/climate-building) identified by Koontz and O'Donnell, along with core principles and traits associated with effective leaders, while noting the limitations of the trait approach. It then covered the classic leadership styles — autocratic, democratic and laissez-faire — and the Managerial Grid of Blake and Mouton, which plots concern for production against concern for people to identify five key leadership styles, with (9,9) Team Management regarded as the ideal. The unit moved to motivation, covering its meaning and importance, and five major theories: Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs, Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory, McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y, McClelland's Need Theory, and Vroom's Expectancy Theory. Finally, it examined communication — its purpose, the sender-to-receiver process (encoding, channel, decoding, feedback, and noise), written, oral and non-verbal forms of communication, the semantic, psychological, organizational, personal and physical barriers that can distort a message, and the distinction between formal communication (following the official chain of command in downward, upward, horizontal and diagonal directions) and informal communication (the spontaneous, unpredictable "grapevine").
