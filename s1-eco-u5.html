0. Introduction to Macroeconomics

Units 1–4 of this course examined microeconomics — the behaviour of individual consumers, firms, and markets. This unit shifts to macroeconomics, which studies the economy as a whole: aggregate output, income, employment, and the overall price level. A manager needs macroeconomic literacy because the demand for almost every firm's product (Unit 1), the cost of borrowing, and the general business climate all move with the broader economy's performance.

1. Macroeconomic Aggregates: GNP and GDP

1.1 Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

GDP is the total market value of all final goods and services produced within the geographical boundaries of a country during a given period (usually a year), regardless of who owns the resources used in production (domestic residents or foreigners).

1.2 Gross National Product (GNP)

GNP is the total market value of all final goods and services produced by the normal residents of a country (using resources owned by them), whether located within the country or abroad, during a given period.

1.3 Relationship Between GNP and GDP

GNP = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA)
where NFIA = (Factor income earned by domestic residents from abroad) − (Factor income earned by foreigners within the domestic economy)

Worked Example: A country's GDP is ₹100 lakh crore. Its residents earn ₹5 lakh crore in factor income from abroad, while foreign residents working within the country earn ₹3 lakh crore.

Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA) = 5 − 3₹2 lakh crore
GNP = GDP + NFIA = 100 + 2₹102 lakh crore

If NFIA is positive (as above), GNP exceeds GDP; if domestic residents earn less abroad than foreigners earn domestically, NFIA is negative and GNP falls short of GDP.

1.4 Nominal vs. Real GDP

  • Nominal GDP — output valued at current year (prevailing) prices, so it can rise simply because prices have risen, even if actual physical output is unchanged.
  • Real GDP — output valued at constant (base-year) prices, isolating the true change in the physical volume of goods and services produced, stripped of the effect of price changes (inflation).
  • GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP) × 100 — a broad price index measuring the average price level of everything included in GDP.

2. Concepts and Measurement of National Income

2.1 The Circular Flow of Income

The circular flow of income illustrates why an economy's total output, total income, and total expenditure are, in principle, all equal to one another — the same total value simply keeps circulating between households and firms.

Fig. 1 — Households supply factor services to firms and receive factor income in return (the upper loop); firms supply goods and services to households, who pay for them with consumption expenditure (the lower loop) — the same rupee value flows continuously in a circle.

2.2 National Income Aggregates

Starting from GNP, a sequence of standard deductions and additions produces the other national income aggregates commonly used by governments and analysts.

Fig. 2 — Each aggregate answers a different question: NNP nets out capital consumed; National Income strips out indirect taxes; Personal Income reflects what households actually receive; Disposable Income is what remains after personal taxes, available to spend or save.

2.3 Methods of Measuring National Income

Fig. 3 — All three approaches should, in principle, yield the same total, since total output equals total income equals total expenditure — the logic behind the circular flow (Fig. 1).

  • Product (Value Added) Method — sums the value added by every producing unit in the economy at every stage of production, carefully avoiding double-counting of intermediate goods.
  • Income Method — sums all factor incomes earned in the economy: rent (for land), wages (for labour), interest (for capital), and profit (for entrepreneurship).
  • Expenditure Method — sums all final expenditure on goods and services: Y = C + I + G + (X − M), where C = private consumption, I = investment, G = government expenditure, and (X − M) = net exports.

2.4 Difficulties in Measuring National Income

  • Non-monetized/informal sector transactions — subsistence farming, unpaid household work, and informal-sector output are difficult to value and often go unrecorded.
  • Risk of double counting — care must be taken to count only the value added at each stage, or only final goods, not intermediate goods.
  • Data and statistical limitations — especially in developing economies, comprehensive and reliable data may not be available.
  • Black money / unreported income — income from illegal or undeclared activities is excluded, understating true national income.

3. Determination of National Income

3.1 The Simple Keynesian (Two-Sector) Model

In the simplest Keynesian model of an economy with only households and firms (no government or foreign trade), national income (Y) is determined by the level of Aggregate Demand (AD), which consists of planned consumption expenditure (C) and planned investment expenditure (I).

Equilibrium Condition: Y = C + I   (equivalently, Saving = Investment, i.e., S = I)

Fig. 4 — Equilibrium income (Y* = 600) occurs exactly where the Aggregate Demand line crosses the 45° line — at any lower income, AD exceeds output and firms respond by producing more; at any higher income, AD falls short and firms cut back.

Worked Example: Consumption function C = 50 + 0.75Y; Investment I = ₹100 crore (autonomous, i.e., independent of income).

Aggregate Demand: AD = C + I = 50 + 0.75Y + 100 = 150 + 0.75Y
Equilibrium condition: Y = AD → Y = 150 + 0.75Y
0.25Y = 150 → Y* = ₹600 crore

Verification: At Y = 600, C = 50 + 0.75(600) = 50 + 450 = ₹500 crore, so Saving S = Y − C = 600 − 500 = ₹100 crore — exactly equal to Investment (I = ₹100 crore), confirming the equilibrium condition S = I.

4. Aggregate Consumption

4.1 Meaning

Aggregate consumption is the total expenditure by all households in an economy on final consumer goods and services during a given period — typically the single largest component of Aggregate Demand (as in Section 3.1) in most economies.

4.2 Keynes' Psychological Law of Consumption

John Maynard Keynes proposed that, as aggregate income rises, aggregate consumption also rises, but by a smaller amount than the rise in income — households tend to save a growing proportion of each additional rupee earned. This "law" is the behavioural foundation of the consumption function examined next.

5. Consumption Function

5.1 Meaning and Linear Form

C = a + bY
where C = consumption expenditure; a = autonomous consumption (consumption that occurs even at zero income, e.g., financed by borrowing or past savings); b = Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC); Y = income.

5.2 Average and Marginal Propensity to Consume

  • Average Propensity to Consume (APC) = Total Consumption ÷ Total Income (C ÷ Y) — the proportion of total income spent on consumption.
  • Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC) = Change in Consumption ÷ Change in Income (ΔC ÷ ΔY) — the proportion of each additional rupee of income that is spent on consumption; this is the slope (b) of the consumption function, and per Keynes' psychological law (Section 4.2), 0 < MPC < 1.

5.3 The Saving Function

S = Y − C = Y − (a + bY) = −a + (1 − b)Y
Marginal Propensity to Save (MPS) = 1 − MPC, and MPC + MPS = 1 always.

Fig. 5 — Below the break-even income of ₹200, households consume more than their income (dis-saving, financed by borrowing or drawing down savings); above it, households save a positive amount, and that saved amount grows with income.

Worked Example (continuing C = 50 + 0.75Y):

6. Numerical Problems Related to Management

Problem 1 (GNP/GDP): A country's GDP is ₹500 crore. Factor income earned by its residents abroad is ₹40 crore, and factor income earned by foreigners within the country is ₹55 crore. Find the country's GNP.
Solution: NFIA = 40 − 55 = −₹15 crore. GNP = GDP + NFIA = 500 + (−15) = ₹485 crore (GNP is less than GDP here, since foreigners earn more domestically than residents earn abroad).

Problem 2 (Equilibrium Income): If C = 80 + 0.6Y and I = ₹120 crore, find the equilibrium level of national income.
Solution: AD = C + I = 80 + 0.6Y + 120 = 200 + 0.6Y. Equilibrium: Y = 200 + 0.6Y → 0.4Y = 200 → Y* = ₹500 crore.

Problem 3 (Consumption Function): If C = 40 + 0.8Y, find the level of saving when income is ₹300.
Solution: C at Y = 300: 40 + 0.8(300) = 40 + 240 = ₹280. S = Y − C = 300 − 280 = ₹20.

Summary

This unit introduced macroeconomics through its core aggregates and models. It defined GDP (output produced within a country's borders) and GNP (output produced by a country's residents, wherever located), linked by Net Factor Income from Abroad, and distinguished nominal from real GDP via the GDP deflator. It explained the circular flow of income between households and firms as the underlying reason why output, income and expenditure are equal, and traced the sequence of national income aggregates from GNP down to Disposable Income through a worked numerical example, alongside the product, income and expenditure methods of measuring national income and the practical difficulties involved. The unit then covered the determination of national income in the simple two-sector Keynesian model, where equilibrium output occurs where Y = C + I (equivalently, S = I), illustrated through the Keynesian cross diagram and a worked example yielding an equilibrium income of ₹600 crore. It introduced aggregate consumption and Keynes' psychological law of consumption, before formalizing this behaviour through the linear consumption function C = a + bY, explaining the Average and Marginal Propensities to Consume, the saving function, and the break-even level of income at which saving is exactly zero — closing with worked numerical problems on GNP calculation, equilibrium income determination, and the consumption/saving function.
