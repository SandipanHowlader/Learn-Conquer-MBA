0. Introduction — Concept, Nature and Importance of Management

0.1 Meaning and Definition of Management

Management is the process by which a group of people combine their efforts and resources to achieve goals they could not achieve individually. It is the invisible force that turns a random collection of people, money, machines and materials into a purposeful, productive organization.

In simple terms, management refers to the process of planning, organizing, staffing, directing and controlling the activities of an enterprise so that predetermined objectives are achieved with the optimum use of available resources. Management is required wherever two or more people work together toward a common goal — in a business firm, a hospital, a school, a government department, or even a household.

Harold Koontz: Management is the art of getting things done through and with people in formally organized groups.

Peter F. Drucker: Management is a multi-purpose organ that manages a business, manages managers, and manages workers and work.

F.W. Taylor: Management is the art of knowing what you want to do and then seeing that it is done in the best and cheapest way.

Mary Parker Follett: Management is the art of getting things done through people.

Henri Fayol: To manage is to forecast and plan, to organize, to command, to co-ordinate and to control.

Taken together, these definitions show that management is (a) a process consisting of interrelated functions, (b) directed toward the achievement of goals, and (c) carried out through and with the cooperation of people.

0.2 Management and Administration — A Note

Some early writers (notably Oliver Sheldon) distinguished "administration" as the top-level, policy-determining function from "management" as the lower-level, execution function. Modern usage, however, treats the two terms as largely synonymous, with "management" being the more widely accepted umbrella term covering both policy formulation and its execution across all levels of the organization.

0.3 Nature / Characteristics of Management

  • Goal-oriented process — management exists only because organizations have goals to achieve.
  • Universal in application — the same broad principles apply to a factory, a hospital, an NGO or a government office.
  • Multidimensional activity — management simultaneously manages work, people and operations.
  • Continuous and dynamic process — it consists of a series of interrelated, ongoing functions that must adapt to a changing environment.
  • Group activity — management is meaningless without a group of people working towards a shared purpose.
  • Intangible force — management itself cannot be seen or touched, but its results (order, coordination, growth) can be felt.
  • Situational — there is no single "best" way to manage; the right approach depends on the circumstances.

0.4 Is Management a Science, an Art, or a Profession?

Basis As a Science As an Art As a Profession
Meaning A systematic body of knowledge based on observation, experimentation and universally applicable principles. Application of personal skill, judgement and creativity to achieve a desired result in practice. An occupation backed by specialized knowledge, formal education, and a code of conduct.
Basis Cause-and-effect relationships, though "soft"/inexact because it deals with unpredictable human behaviour. Practical knowledge, personal skill, and continuous practice. Formal education (e.g., MBA), representative bodies (e.g., AIMA), and ethical standards.
Verdict An inexact / social science. Definitely an art — "the art of getting things done through people." An emerging profession, though entry is not yet legally restricted as in law or medicine.

Conclusion: Management is best regarded as both a science and an art — a science because it rests on principles built through study and experience, and an art because applying those principles skilfully requires creativity and practice. It is also a fast-emerging profession.

0.5 Importance of Management

  • Achievement of group goals — creates teamwork and channels individual effort toward a common objective.
  • Optimum utilization of resources — ensures the best use of men, money, materials and machines, minimizing wastage.
  • Reduction of costs — effective planning and control help maximize output with minimum input.
  • Establishes a sound organization — clarifies authority-responsibility relationships and avoids duplication of work.
  • Helps the organization adapt to change — enables the enterprise to cope with a dynamic technological, economic and social environment.
  • Provides innovation — encourages new ideas, products and processes that keep the organization competitive.
  • Contributes to the development of the nation — efficient management generates employment, wealth and an improved standard of living for society at large.

1. Functions of Management

Every manager, regardless of level or type of organization, performs the same set of interrelated functions. These are widely taught using the acronym POSDC (Planning, Organizing, Staffing, Directing, Controlling), built on the foundation laid by Henri Fayol.

Fig. 1 — Planning, Organizing, Staffing, Directing and Controlling are not one-time steps but a repeating cycle: control findings feed back into the next round of planning.

1.1 Planning

Planning is the primary function of management — deciding in advance what to do, how to do it, when to do it, and who is to do it. It bridges the gap between where the organization is today and where it wants to be, and includes setting objectives, forecasting, formulating policies, procedures and budgets.

1.2 Organizing

Organizing is the process of identifying and grouping the work to be performed, defining and delegating authority and responsibility, and establishing relationships to enable people to work together most effectively. It results in the creation of a formal structure of tasks and authority.

1.3 Staffing

Staffing involves manning the organizational structure through the recruitment, selection, training, development, appraisal and compensation of personnel. It is often called the human-resource function of management because it ensures the right people are in the right jobs.

1.4 Directing (Leading)

Directing means guiding, motivating, supervising and communicating with subordinates so that they willingly contribute to organizational goals. It has four sub-elements:

  • Supervision
  • Motivation
  • Leadership
  • Communication

1.5 Controlling

Controlling is the process of measuring actual performance against planned standards, identifying deviations, and taking corrective action. It involves setting standards, measuring performance, comparing it with the standard, and correcting deviations to keep the organization on track.

Function Core Question Answered Key Output
PlanningWhat is to be done and how?Objectives, policies, budgets, schedules
OrganizingWho does what, with what authority?Structure, division of work, delegation
StaffingWho will fill each role?Recruitment, training, appraisal systems
DirectingHow is work carried out on a daily basis?Supervision, motivation, communication
ControllingIs performance on track?Standards, measurement, corrective action

Note: Some texts also treat Coordinating as a distinct function — the synchronizing of individual and departmental efforts to ensure unity of action — since it is essential to the smooth working of all the other functions.

2. Managerial Functions at Different Organizational Levels

While every manager performs the functions described above, the emphasis given to each function varies considerably by hierarchical level.

2.1 Top-Level Management

Comprising the Board of Directors, Chairman, Managing Director, CEO and President, top management concentrates mainly on planning and organizing. It sets the overall mission, objectives and strategy, is answerable to shareholders and society, and deals extensively with the external environment.

2.2 Middle-Level Management

Departmental heads, divisional managers and branch managers focus chiefly on organizing and directing. They translate the broad policies of top management into departmental plans, coordinate the work of different sections, and act as the vital communication link between top and lower management.

2.3 Lower-Level (Supervisory) Management

Foremen, supervisors and section officers focus mainly on directing and controlling. They deal directly with workers on the shop floor, ensure the quality and quantity of output, maintain discipline, and report operational problems upward.

3. Managerial Skills and Levels of Management

3.1 Robert L. Katz's Managerial Skills

Conceptual Skills: The ability to view the organization as a whole, understand how its parts interrelate, and think in abstract and strategic terms. Most critical for top management.

Human (Interpersonal) Skills: The ability to work with, understand, and motivate people, both individually and in groups. Equally important at every level of management.

Technical Skills: Specialized knowledge and proficiency in a particular field, method or process. Most critical for lower/supervisory management, closest to day-to-day operations.

Some later writers add a fourth skill — Design Skills, the ability not just to identify a problem but to design a workable solution that benefits the organization.

3.2 Katz's Skill-Level Matrix

Level of Management Conceptual Skills Human Skills Technical Skills
Top ManagementHighModerate–HighLow
Middle ManagementModerateHighModerate
Lower ManagementLowHighHigh

As one moves up the hierarchy, the need for conceptual skill rises while the need for technical skill falls; human skill remains uniformly important at every level.

3.3 Levels of Management — Recap

  • Top-Level Management: Board of Directors, Chairman, Managing Director, CEO.
  • Middle-Level Management: Divisional/Departmental Managers, Branch Managers.
  • Lower-Level Management: Supervisors, Foremen, Section Leaders.

4. Goals, Productivity, Effectiveness and Efficiency

4.1 Goals

Goals are the desired future outcomes an organization strives to achieve. They provide direction, motivate employees, and serve as a benchmark against which performance can be evaluated. Goals may be classified as strategic (set by top management), tactical (set by middle management), or operational (set by lower management).

4.2 Productivity

Productivity is the ratio of output to input over a given period, with due regard to quality:

Productivity = Output ÷ Input

It measures how well an organization converts resources — labour, capital, materials, time — into goods and services. Higher productivity means more (or better-quality) output from the same or fewer inputs.

4.3 Effectiveness

Effectiveness means "doing the right things" — achieving the stated goals or intended results, irrespective of the resources consumed in the process. It is an output-centred, results-focused measure.

4.4 Efficiency

Efficiency means "doing things right" — achieving goals while using the minimum possible resources, avoiding wastage of time, money and materials. It is an input-centred, process-focused measure.

4.5 Relationship between Effectiveness and Efficiency

Low Efficiency High Efficiency
High Effectiveness Goals achieved, but resources wasted Goals achieved with optimum resource use — the ideal position
Low Effectiveness Goals not achieved and resources wasted — the worst position Resources well used, but the wrong goals are pursued

Fig. 4 — The top-right quadrant, where goals are achieved (effectiveness) using minimal resources (efficiency), is what every manager aims for.

As Peter Drucker summarized: efficiency is doing things right; effectiveness is doing the right things. A good manager must aim to be both effective and efficient at the same time.

5. Mintzberg's Managerial Roles Approach

Based on an empirical study of chief executives at work, Henry Mintzberg concluded that a manager's job cannot be fully captured by the classical functions alone. Instead, he identified ten roles, grouped into three categories.

5.1 Interpersonal Roles

Arise from the manager's formal authority and status and involve relationships with people:

  • Figurehead — performs ceremonial and symbolic duties (greeting visitors, signing documents).
  • Leader — motivates, directs and guides subordinates.
  • Liaison — maintains a network of contacts outside the direct chain of command.

5.2 Informational Roles

Involve receiving, collecting and disseminating information:

  • Monitor — scans the environment for information affecting the organization.
  • Disseminator — passes information received from outsiders/subordinates on to organization members.
  • Spokesperson — transmits information to outsiders about the organization's plans, policies and results.

5.3 Decisional Roles

Involve making significant decisions affecting the organization:

  • Entrepreneur — initiates change and identifies new opportunities.
  • Disturbance Handler — takes corrective action during unexpected crises or conflicts.
  • Resource Allocator — decides where organizational resources will be applied.
  • Negotiator — represents the organization in major negotiations.
Category Roles Underlying Basis
InterpersonalFigurehead, Leader, LiaisonFormal authority and status
InformationalMonitor, Disseminator, SpokespersonAccess to information
DecisionalEntrepreneur, Disturbance Handler, Resource Allocator, NegotiatorAccess to information and authority

Fig. 3 — Mintzberg grouped a manager's day-to-day work into ten roles nested under three broad categories, rather than a neat planning-to-controlling sequence.

Significance: Mintzberg's findings challenged the traditional (Fayol-style) view that a manager's work is neatly sequential. He showed that actual managerial work is fragmented, fast-paced and driven by these ten interrelated roles rather than a tidy cycle of planning-organizing-directing-controlling.

6. Management Process or Operational Approach

The Management Process (Operational) Approach, associated chiefly with Henri Fayol and later systematized by Harold Koontz and Cyril O'Donnell, treats management as a universal process built around a core set of functions.

6.1 Core Idea

Management is the process of getting things done through and with people, performed by every manager regardless of the type or size of the enterprise. This process consists of the interrelated, universally applicable functions of Planning, Organizing, Staffing, Directing and Controlling. The approach draws selectively on other disciplines — sociology, psychology, economics, mathematics — while keeping the core management functions at its centre.

6.2 Key Features

  • Views management as a universal process, applicable across all kinds of organizations.
  • Focuses on the functions managers perform, rather than on their personal traits.
  • Builds a body of management theory through observation, experience and logical analysis.
  • Treats management as a science that borrows from, but is not subordinate to, other fields of knowledge.

Koontz famously described the many conflicting schools of management theory as the "Management Theory Jungle" and proposed the operational/process approach as the framework best able to organize this jungle into a coherent, teachable body of knowledge.

6.3 Significance

Because it is practical, systematic, and easily applied across industries and levels, the operational approach (planning → organizing → staffing → directing → controlling) remains the most widely used framework for teaching the principles of management.

7. Development of Management Thought

7.1 Pre-Scientific Era

Before the twentieth century, management practices existed in military organizations, the church, and family-run trading houses, but were not studied systematically. The Industrial Revolution (18th–19th century) created large factories and complex production processes, generating the need for a systematic study of management.

7.2 Classical Approach

The classical approach concentrates on structure, formal organization, and efficiency, and largely views the worker as an "economic man" motivated chiefly by monetary reward. It comprises three streams:

Stream Key Contributor Focus Core Contribution
Scientific Management F.W. Taylor (early 1900s) Shop-floor / operational efficiency Time and motion study, differential piece-rate system, functional foremanship, standardization
Administrative Management Henri Fayol (1916) Organization as a whole; top-management functions Five functions of management and 14 general principles (see Section 8)
Bureaucratic Management Max Weber Ideal-type formal organization Division of labour, hierarchy, formal rules, impersonality, merit-based selection

Taylor's four principles of scientific management: (1) develop a science for each element of work in place of rule-of-thumb methods; (2) scientifically select, train and develop workers; (3) cooperate with workers to ensure the work is done in accordance with scientific principles; (4) divide work and responsibility almost equally between management and workers.

7.3 Neo-Classical Approach (Human Relations Approach)

The neo-classical approach emerged as a reaction to the classical school's neglect of psychological and social factors. It views the worker as a "social man", influenced strongly by group norms, sentiments and informal relationships rather than by pay alone.

Elton Mayo and the Hawthorne Studies (1924–1932), conducted at the Western Electric Company's Hawthorne Plant, are the foundation of this approach. Key findings include:
  • The Hawthorne Effect — worker productivity rose whenever workers were given attention, largely independent of actual changes in physical working conditions.
  • The powerful influence of informal groups and their norms on individual worker behaviour.
  • Social and psychological factors — recognition, participation, communication — affect productivity as much as, or more than, purely physical or monetary factors.

Behavioural scientists building on this foundation include Abraham Maslow (Hierarchy of Needs), Douglas McGregor (Theory X and Theory Y), and Chris Argyris, who together broadened the human-relations school into the wider Behavioural Science Approach.

7.4 Modern Approaches

  • Quantitative / Management Science Approach — applies mathematical models, statistics and operations research (e.g., linear programming, queuing theory) to managerial decision-making, especially in planning and control.
  • Systems Approach — views the organization as an open system made up of interrelated sub-systems (input → process → output → feedback) continuously interacting with its external environment.
  • Contingency (Situational) Approach — holds that there is no single "best" way to manage; the right technique depends on the specific situation the organization faces (an "it depends" approach).
  • Operational / Management Process Approach — (see Section 6) synthesizes elements of the other schools around the core functions of management.

8. Fayol's Management Theory

Henri Fayol (1841–1925), a French mining engineer and industrialist, is regarded as the "Father of Modern Management Theory." His ideas, published in General and Industrial Management (1916), focused on the administrative activities of top management and remain the basis of the functional approach taught today.

8.1 Fayol's Six Groups of Industrial Activities

  • Technical — production and manufacture.
  • Commercial — buying, selling and exchange.
  • Financial — search for and optimum use of capital.
  • Security — protection of property and persons.
  • Accounting — record-keeping, costing and statistics.
  • Managerial — planning, organizing, commanding, coordinating and controlling.

Fayol observed that managerial ability becomes increasingly important as one rises up the organizational hierarchy, while technical ability is most important at the operating level.

8.2 Fayol's Five Elements of Management

  1. Planning (Prevoyance) — examining the future and drawing up a plan of action.
  2. Organizing — building up the material and human structure of the undertaking.
  3. Commanding — directing and leading personnel.
  4. Coordinating — unifying and harmonizing all activities and efforts.
  5. Controlling — ensuring everything occurs in accordance with established rules and instructions.

These five elements are the direct ancestor of the modern Planning–Organizing–Staffing–Directing–Controlling framework taught in Section 1.

8.3 Fayol's 14 Principles of Management

No. Principle Explanation
1Division of WorkSpecialization increases efficiency by dividing work among individuals according to their expertise.
2Authority and ResponsibilityAuthority (the right to give orders) must go hand-in-hand with corresponding responsibility.
3DisciplineObedience, application, energy and respect for the agreements between employees and the organization.
4Unity of CommandEach employee should receive orders from only one superior to avoid confusion.
5Unity of DirectionEach group of activities with the same objective should be directed by one manager using one plan.
6Subordination of Individual to General InterestThe interests of the organization must prevail over individual or personal interests.
7RemunerationCompensation should be fair and satisfactory to both employees and the organization.
8CentralizationThe degree of concentration of decision-making authority at the top should suit the situation.
9Scalar ChainThe formal line of authority from the highest to the lowest rank; a "Gang Plank" allows lateral communication in urgent cases.

8.4 Significance and Criticism of Fayol's Theory

Contributions: Fayol provided the first systematic, comprehensive theory of general management applicable to all types of organizations, laid the foundation for the functions of management taught today, and emphasized that management is a distinct, teachable skill separate from technical expertise.

Criticisms: The principles rest largely on Fayol's personal experience rather than rigorous empirical testing; some principles (e.g., unity of command) sit uneasily with modern flexible or matrix structures; the theory pays little attention to the human/behavioural and informal aspects of organizations, later addressed by the Neo-Classical school; and the principles can feel too general or rigid for the complexity of today's dynamic organizations.

Summary

This unit introduced the foundational framework of management as both a discipline and a practical activity. It began with the concept, nature and importance of management, and examined whether management is best understood as a science, an art, or a profession. It then discussed the five core functions of management — planning, organizing, staffing, directing and controlling — and showed how the emphasis on each function shifts across the top, middle and lower levels of the organizational hierarchy. The unit explained Robert Katz's classification of conceptual, human and technical skills and their varying importance at each management level, and distinguished between goals, productivity, effectiveness and efficiency as measures of managerial performance. It further explored Henry Mintzberg's ten managerial roles, grouped into interpersonal, informational and decisional categories, as an alternative lens on what managers actually do, and outlined the Management Process (Operational) Approach associated with Fayol and Koontz. Finally, the unit traced the historical development of management thought through the classical school (Taylor, Fayol, Weber), the neo-classical/human-relations school (Mayo, Maslow, McGregor), and the modern quantitative, systems and contingency approaches, closing with a detailed study of Henri Fayol's six industrial activities, five elements of management, and fourteen principles of management.
