Introduction to Management

Read Time: 45 mins

1. Concept, Nature, and Importance of Management

1.1 Concept of Management

Management is the process of planning, organizing, staffing, directing, and controlling organizational resources (human, financial, physical, and informational) to achieve predetermined goals efficiently and effectively.

Key Definitions:
  • Harold Koontz: "Management is the art of getting things done through and with people in formally organized groups."
  • Peter Drucker: "Management is a multi-purpose organ that manages business, manages managers, and manages workers and work."
  • F.W. Taylor: "Management is the art of knowing what you want to do and then seeing that it is done in the best and cheapest way."
  • Mary Parker Follett: "Management is the art of getting things done through people."

Management, therefore, is both a process (a series of continuous, interrelated activities) and a discipline (a systematic body of knowledge).

1.2 Nature of Management

Management as a field of study has the following characteristics:

  • Goal-Oriented Process – Every organization has goals, and management is the process undertaken to achieve them.
  • Universal Process – Management principles apply to all types of organizations (business, government, hospitals, educational institutions) and at all levels.
  • Multidimensional Activity – Management involves management of work, people, and operations simultaneously.
  • Continuous Process – Management is an ongoing process consisting of a series of interrelated functions.
  • Group Activity – Management involves working with and through people organized into groups.
  • Dynamic Function – Management must adapt to a changing environment (economic, social, political, technological).
  • Intangible Force – Management cannot be seen but its presence can be felt through the results (order, coordination, productivity) it produces.
  • Management as an Art, Science, and Profession – Discussed in detail below.

Is Management a Science, an Art, or a Profession?

Aspect Explanation
Science Management has a systematic body of knowledge, universally applicable principles derived through observation and experimentation. However, it is a "soft" or social science since it deals with human behavior, which is unpredictable — hence called an inexact science.
Art Art involves the practical application of knowledge and skills to achieve desired results. Management requires personal skill, creativity, and practice, making it an art — specifically, the art of getting things done through people.
Profession A profession requires specialized knowledge, formal education, a representative body, and a code of conduct. Management is emerging as a profession (e.g., MBA degrees, bodies like AIMA), though it does not yet have entry restrictions like medicine or law.

Conclusion: Management is both a science and an art — it uses scientific methods to build knowledge (science) and applies that knowledge with skill and creativity (art). It is also increasingly recognized as a profession.

1.3 Importance of Management

  • Achievement of Group Goals – Creates a team spirit and coordinates individual efforts toward common objectives.
  • Optimum Utilization of Resources – Ensures the best use of physical, financial, and human resources, reducing wastage.
  • Reduces Costs – Through effective planning, organizing, and control, management minimizes costs and maximizes output.
  • Establishes Sound Organization – Defines authority-responsibility relationships, avoiding duplication of effort.
  • Establishes Equilibrium – Helps organizations adapt to changes in the external environment (technology, market, government policy).
  • Essential for Prosperity of Society – Efficient management leads to economic growth, better quality of goods/services, employment generation, and improved standard of living.
  • Change Management – Helps organizations foresee and manage change through innovation and improvement.
  • Development of the Nation – Good management is a key input for economic and social development.

2. Functions of Management

The functions of management are commonly grouped under the acronym POSDCORB (Luther Gulick) or more commonly taught as five core functions, based on Henri Fayol's and later theorists' work:

2.1 Planning

Deciding in advance what to do, how to do it, when to do it, and who is to do it. It bridges the gap between where we are and where we want to be. Involves setting objectives, forecasting, formulating policies, and preparing schedules/budgets.

2.2 Organizing

The process of identifying and grouping activities, defining and delegating authority and responsibility, and establishing relationships to enable people to work most effectively together. Includes division of work, departmentalization, and establishing hierarchy.

2.3 Staffing

Manning the organization structure through proper recruitment, selection, training, development, promotion, and compensation of personnel. Often called the "human resource function" of management.

2.4 Directing (Leading)

Guiding, motivating, supervising, and communicating with subordinates so they contribute effectively to organizational goals. It includes four sub-elements:

  • Supervision
  • Motivation
  • Leadership
  • Communication

2.5 Controlling

The process of measuring actual performance against planned targets, identifying deviations, and taking corrective action. Involves:

  • Setting standards
  • Measuring actual performance
  • Comparing with standards
  • Correcting deviations

Note: Some texts also add Coordinating as a separate function (especially in the classical approach) since coordination is required to synchronize the efforts of individuals/departments to achieve unity of action.

Diagrammatic Flow:
Planning → Organizing → Staffing → Directing → Controlling
(Continuous, cyclical, and interrelated process)

3. Managerial Functions at Different Organizational Levels

Management functions are performed differently depending on the level of management in the hierarchy:

3.1 Top-Level Management (Board of Directors, CEO, President, GM)

  • Focuses primarily on Planning and Organizing
  • Determines overall objectives, policies, and strategies
  • Responsible for the welfare and survival of the organization
  • Interacts with the external environment (government, shareholders, society)
  • Long-term/strategic decision-making

3.2 Middle-Level Management (Departmental Heads, Branch Managers)

  • Focuses on Organizing and Directing
  • Implements policies framed by top management
  • Coordinates activities of different sections/departments
  • Acts as a link between top and lower management
  • Medium-term/tactical decision-making

3.3 Lower-Level / Supervisory Management (Foremen, Supervisors, Section Officers)

  • Focuses primarily on Directing and Controlling
  • Directly supervises workers and day-to-day operations
  • Ensures work quality and discipline
  • Short-term/operational decision-making
  • Maintains a link between workers and middle management

Summary Table

Level Primary Functions Nature of Decisions Time Horizon
Top Planning, Organizing Strategic Long-term
Middle Organizing, Directing Tactical Medium-term
Lower/Supervisory Directing, Controlling Operational Short-term

4. Managerial Skills and Levels of Management

4.1 Robert L. Katz's Managerial Skills

Robert Katz identified three essential skills needed by managers:

  • 1. Conceptual Skills – The ability to see the organization as a whole, understand complex relationships, and think in abstract terms. Essential for top management in strategic decision-making.
  • 2. Human (Interpersonal) Skills – The ability to work with, understand, motivate, and lead people, both individually and in groups. Equally important at all levels of management.
  • 3. Technical Skills – Specialized knowledge and proficiency in a specific field (e.g., accounting, engineering, marketing techniques). Most important at the lower/supervisory level, where hands-on knowledge of the job is required.

Some texts add a fourth skill:
4. Design Skills – The ability to solve problems in ways that benefit the organization, not just identify problems.

4.2 Katz's Skill-Level Matrix

Level of Management Conceptual Skills Human Skills Technical Skills
Top Management High Moderate-High Low
Middle Management Moderate High Moderate
Lower Management Low High High

(Illustrated as an inverted/direct pyramid: conceptual skill needs decrease going down the hierarchy while technical skill needs increase.)

4.3 Levels of Management (Recap)

  1. Top-Level Management – Board of Directors, Chairman, Managing Director, CEO
  2. Middle-Level Management – Divisional/Departmental Managers
  3. Lower-Level Management – Supervisors, Foremen, Section Leaders

5. Goals, Productivity, Effectiveness, and Efficiency

5.1 Goals

Goals are the desired future outcomes that an organization strives to achieve. They provide direction, motivate employees, and act as a benchmark for evaluating performance. Goals can be classified as:

  • Strategic goals (set by top management)
  • Tactical goals (set by middle management)
  • Operational goals (set by lower management)

5.2 Productivity

Productivity is the ratio of output to input in a given time period, with due consideration to quality:

Productivity = Output / Input

It reflects how well resources (labor, capital, materials) are combined and utilized. High productivity means more output is achieved with the same or fewer inputs.

5.3 Effectiveness

Effectiveness means "doing the right things" — achieving the stated goals or desired outcomes, regardless of the resources consumed. It focuses on the end result.

5.4 Efficiency

Efficiency means "doing things right" — achieving goals with the minimum use of resources (least wastage of time, money, and materials). It focuses on the input-output relationship.

5.5 Relationship Between Effectiveness and Efficiency

Low Efficiency High Efficiency
High Effectiveness Goals achieved but with wastage of resources Goals achieved with optimum resource use (Ideal situation)
Low Effectiveness Resources wasted and goals not achieved (Worst situation) Resources well used but goals not achieved

Peter Drucker's View: "Efficiency is doing things right; effectiveness is doing the right things." A good manager must aim to be both effective and efficient — achieving the correct goals while making optimal use of resources.

6. Mintzberg's Managerial Roles Approach

Henry Mintzberg, through an empirical study of chief executives, concluded that managers perform ten different, highly interrelated roles, grouped into three categories:

6.1 Interpersonal Roles

Arise from the manager's formal authority and status, and involve relationships with people:

  1. Figurehead – Performing ceremonial and symbolic duties (e.g., greeting visitors, signing documents)
  2. Leader – Motivating, directing, and guiding subordinates
  3. Liaison – Maintaining a network of contacts outside the vertical chain of command (with peers, external stakeholders)

6.2 Informational Roles

Involve receiving, collecting, and disseminating information:

  1. Monitor – Scanning the environment for information affecting the organization
  2. Disseminator – Transmitting information received from outsiders/subordinates to members of the organization
  3. Spokesperson – Transmitting information to outsiders about organizational plans, policies, and results

6.3 Decisional Roles

Involve making significant decisions that affect the organization:

  1. Entrepreneur – Initiating change and innovation, identifying new opportunities
  2. Disturbance Handler – Taking corrective action during unexpected crises/conflicts
  3. Resource Allocator – Deciding where organizational resources will be applied
  4. Negotiator – Representing the organization in major negotiations (with unions, suppliers, etc.)

Significance: Mintzberg's approach challenged the traditional view (Fayol's) that management is simply planning, organizing, directing, and controlling. He argued that a manager's actual day-to-day work is fragmented, varied, and driven by these ten roles rather than neat sequential functions.

7. Management Process or Operational Approach

The Management Process (Operational) Approach, primarily associated with Henri Fayol and later developed by Harold Koontz and Cyril O'Donnell, views management as a universal process consisting of certain identifiable functions.

7.1 Core Idea

  • Management is a process of getting things done through and with people, performed by all managers regardless of enterprise or level.
  • The process consists of primary functions — Planning, Organizing, Staffing, Directing, and Controlling — which are interrelated and universally applicable.
  • Draws upon concepts, principles, and techniques from other fields (sociology, psychology, economics, etc.) but organizes them around the central core of management functions.

7.2 Key Features of the Operational Approach

  1. Management is viewed as a universal process, applicable to all kinds of organizations.
  2. It focuses on the functions performed by managers, rather than on the manager's personal traits.
  3. It develops a body of management theory built through observation, experience, and logic.
  4. It considers management as a science that draws on other disciplines while retaining its own core.
  5. Koontz called the vast, conflicting body of management theories the "Management Theory Jungle" and advocated the process approach as a way to synthesize them into a coherent framework.

7.3 Significance

This approach is the most widely used in teaching management principles because it is practical, systematic, and provides a clear framework (planning → organizing → staffing → directing → controlling) applicable across industries and levels.

8. Development of Management Thought

Management thought has evolved through several overlapping phases:

8.1 Pre-Scientific / Early Contributions

Before the 20th century, management practices existed but were not systematized. Early influences came from military organizations, the church, and family-run enterprises. The Industrial Revolution (18th–19th century) created the need for systematic management as factories grew in size and complexity.

8.2 Classical Approach

Focuses on structure, efficiency, and formal organization; treats workers largely as economic beings ("Economic Man" concept). It has three main branches:

  • (a) Scientific Management (F.W. Taylor, early 1900s): Focuses on shop-floor/operational efficiency. Key Principles: Science, not rule of thumb; Harmony, not discord; Cooperation, not individualism; Development of each worker to their greatest efficiency. Techniques: Time and motion study, differential piece-rate system, functional foremanship, standardization of tools and methods.
  • (b) Administrative Management (Henri Fayol, 1916): Focuses on the organization as a whole, and the functions/principles applicable to top management.
  • (c) Bureaucratic Management (Max Weber): Focuses on an "ideal type" organization based on clear division of labor, hierarchy of authority, formal rules and procedures, impersonality (decisions based on rules, not personal relationships), and selection and promotion based on merit/qualification.

8.3 Neo-Classical Approach (Human Relations Approach)

Emerged as a reaction to the classical approach's neglect of human/psychological factors. Focused on the "Social Man" concept — workers as social beings influenced by group norms, sentiments, and informal relationships.

  • Elton Mayo and the Hawthorne Studies (1924–1932) at the Western Electric Company's Hawthorne Plant are the foundation of this approach. Key findings include the Hawthorne Effect (workers' productivity increased when they were given attention), the importance of informal groups and their norms, and that social and psychological factors affect productivity as much as physical/monetary factors.
  • Behavioral scientists such as Abraham Maslow (Hierarchy of Needs), Douglas McGregor (Theory X and Theory Y), and Chris Argyris further developed this human-behavior focus into the broader Behavioral Science Approach.

8.4 Modern Approaches to Management

Modern management thought moves beyond a single "best way," recognizing complexity and situational variation:

  1. Quantitative / Management Science Approach – Uses mathematical models, statistics, and operations research (e.g., linear programming, queuing theory) for decision-making, especially in planning and control.
  2. Systems Approach – Views the organization as an open system composed of interrelated and interdependent sub-systems (input–process–output–feedback), interacting continuously with the external environment.
  3. Contingency (Situational) Approach – Argues there is no single best way to manage; the appropriate management technique depends on the specific situation, environment, and circumstances faced by the organization ("it depends" approach).
  4. Operational/Management Process Approach – Synthesizes elements of other approaches around the core functions of management.

9. Fayol's Management Theory

Henri Fayol (1841–1925), a French mining engineer and industrialist, is regarded as the "Father of Modern Management Theory" (as opposed to Taylor, who is the "Father of Scientific Management"). His theory, presented in "General and Industrial Management" (1916), focused on the administrative activities of top management.

9.1 Fayol's Classification of Business Activities

Fayol divided all industrial activities into six groups:

  1. Technical (production)
  2. Commercial (buying, selling, exchange)
  3. Financial (search for and optimal use of capital)
  4. Security (protection of property and persons)
  5. Accounting (record-keeping, costing, statistics)
  6. Managerial (planning, organizing, commanding, coordinating, controlling)

Fayol emphasized that managerial ability becomes increasingly important as one moves up the organizational hierarchy.

9.2 Fayol's Five Elements/Functions of Management

  1. Planning (Prevoyance) – Examining the future and drawing up a plan of action
  2. Organizing – Building up the structure (material and human) of the undertaking
  3. Commanding – Directing and leading personnel
  4. Coordinating – Unifying and harmonizing all activities and efforts
  5. Controlling – Ensuring everything occurs according to established rules and instructions

9.3 Fayol's 14 Principles of Management

No. Principle Explanation
1Division of WorkSpecialization increases efficiency and output by dividing work among individuals according to expertise.
2Authority and ResponsibilityAuthority (the right to give orders) must be accompanied by corresponding responsibility (accountability).
3DisciplineObedience, application, energy, and respect for agreements between the organization and its employees.
4Unity of CommandEach employee should receive orders from only one superior to avoid confusion.
5Unity of DirectionEach group of activities with the same objective should be directed by one manager using one plan.
6Subordination of Individual Interest to General InterestThe interests of the organization should prevail over individual/personal interests.
7RemunerationCompensation should be fair, satisfactory, and rewarding to both employees and the organization.

9Scalar ChainThe formal line of authority/communication from the highest to the lowest rank ("Gang Plank" allows lateral communication in urgent cases).
10OrderA place for everything (and everyone) and everything (and everyone) in its place — material and social order.

9.4 Significance and Criticism of Fayol's Theory

Contributions:

  • First systematic, comprehensive theory of general management applicable to all organizations.
  • Laid the foundation for the functions of management taught today.
  • Emphasized that management can be taught (management is a distinct skill, separate from technical skill).

Criticisms:

  • Principles are based largely on Fayol's personal experience, lacking rigorous scientific validation.
  • Some principles (e.g., unity of command) conflict with modern flexible/matrix organizational structures.
  • Neglects the human/behavioral and informal aspects of organizations (later addressed by the Neo-Classical approach).
  • Principles are sometimes too general/rigid for the complexity of modern dynamic organizations.

10. Quick Revision Summary

  • Management = getting work done through people; is a science, an art, and an emerging profession.
  • Functions: Planning, Organizing, Staffing, Directing, Controlling (POSDC).
  • Levels: Top (planning/organizing) → Middle (organizing/directing) → Lower (directing/controlling).
  • Katz's Skills: Conceptual (top) > Human (all levels) > Technical (lower level).
  • Efficiency = doing things right (input focus); Effectiveness = doing right things (output/goal focus).
  • Mintzberg's 10 Roles = Interpersonal (3) + Informational (3) + Decisional (4).
  • Operational Approach = universal process view of management (Fayol, Koontz).
  • Evolution: Classical (Taylor, Fayol, Weber) → Neo-Classical/Human Relations (Mayo, Maslow, McGregor) → Modern (Quantitative, Systems, Contingency).
  • Fayol = Father of Modern Management; 6 activities, 5 functions, 14 principles.
